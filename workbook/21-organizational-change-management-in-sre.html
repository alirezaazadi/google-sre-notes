Organizational Change Management in SRE

Large reliability changes are migrations of people and systems, not just better code. This chapter uses change-management models and a Waze case study to show how urgency, coalition building, experiments, communication, training, migration cost, and reinforcement determine whether a technically good design becomes the new normal.

01 — distinction

Change management is not change control

SRE already cares about technical change control—safe deployments, reviews, rollback, error budgets. Organizational change management asks a different question: how do you move people, processes, ownership, and incentives from one operating model to another?

A migration can be technically correct yet fail because teams do not understand the value, cannot afford the switching cost, lack training, or do not trust the new owner/system.

02 — models

Use frameworks as lenses, not commandments

The chapter surveys several models rather than declaring one universal answer. Lewin frames change as unfreeze → change → refreeze. McKinsey 7-S reminds you that systems, structure, skills, staff, strategy, style, and shared values interact. Kotter emphasizes urgency, coalition, vision, removing barriers, short-term wins, sustaining acceleration, and anchoring the result.

ADKAR focuses on the individual path: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. PDCA captures iterative Plan → Do → Check → Act improvement. The useful model depends on whether the obstacle is motivation, organization alignment, technical uncertainty, or execution.

ModelUseful question
LewinHave people left the old equilibrium, and will the new behavior stick?
7-SWhich organizational dimensions are misaligned with the technical change?
KotterDo we have urgency, coalition, visible wins, and a way to sustain momentum?
ADKARDoes each affected person know why, want it, know how, and have ability/support?
PDCACan we make the migration an evidence-driven iteration loop?
03 — waze

Waze: operational pain creates real urgency

Waze’s messaging/queue infrastructure reached a point where the system could not keep up with growth and engineers were intervening increasingly often. The situation eventually became a near-continuous operational burden—effectively 24/7 firefighting. That is genuine urgency: the current design is no longer economically or operationally sustainable.

The team still did not jump straight to one rewrite. It built a coalition and collected solution proposals—more than 65 candidate ideas were narrowed to a small set worth deeper investigation. That process broadened ownership of the solution and reduced the risk of one team imposing an architecture based only on local assumptions.

04 — migration

A better system must be adoptable

Migration cost matters. Compatibility layers, automated conversion, documentation, training, incremental cutovers, and concrete early wins can make the new path cheap enough that teams actually move.

A big-bang migration that requires every customer to stop feature work for months before receiving any benefit is difficult to sustain politically and operationally. Small wins demonstrate credibility and reveal design flaws while the blast radius is manageable.

05 — people

Virtual teams expose coordination cost

The chapter also discusses the friction of temporary/virtual teams. One effort found participants spending around 80% of their time on the cross-organizational initiative, which created tension with their home-team responsibilities. A change plan must therefore account for real staffing and priority conflicts, not assume contributors have free capacity.

Psychological and incentive dimensions matter: teams need permission to spend time migrating, confidence that the new platform will be supported, and clarity about who owns problems during transition.

06 — reinforce

Finish the migration

Measure adoption and reliability outcomes. Update documentation, ownership, training, and standard processes. Eventually retire the old path when safe; indefinite dual operation preserves the complexity and toil the migration was meant to remove.

The chapter's deeper point is that adoption is part of engineering. If your technically elegant platform cannot cross the social/operational migration path, it has not solved the organization’s reliability problem.

Simple adoption equation: visible value must outweigh migration cost, and teams must have the authority and capacity to make the change.
