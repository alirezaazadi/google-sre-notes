Google SRE Workbook · Chapter 8

On-Call

The Workbook revisits on-call for organizations of different sizes and maturity. It focuses on the practical design of rotations, response-time expectations, reducing noisy paging, transferring knowledge, using data to prioritize paging fixes, and keeping both humans and services healthy.

01 — purpose

On-call connects an availability promise to a human response

An on-call rotation exists because some service failures cannot yet be fully repaired by automation. The responder must be reachable within the response time implied by the service’s reliability requirements.

Different incidents justify different response times. The Workbook gives examples ranging from a revenue-impacting network outage requiring a response within minutes, to a stuck batch system tolerating tens of minutes, to a pre-launch backup issue that can simply create a business-hours ticket.

This distinction is crucial. If every abnormal condition receives the strictest response requirement, on-call becomes unnecessarily restrictive and the pager becomes noisy.

02 — rotations

Rotation design depends on staffing and service needs

There is no single Google-sized rotation that every company must copy. The chapter addresses small teams, mixed developer/SRE rotations, multi-site models, and teams with frequent turnover.

Primary/secondary responsibilities should be explicit. A secondary can be page fallback, can own nonurgent production work, or can provide specialized support. If roles are ambiguous, work is duplicated or silently dropped.

Response-time commitments affect how constrained a person is during a shift. A five-minute response requirement effectively requires immediate access to an authenticated laptop and reliable network; a thirty-minute requirement allows more freedom.

Healthy rotation design also needs rest, handoff, compensation or time-off practices appropriate to the organization, and a clear escalation path.

Key idea: the on-call contract includes both the service expectation and the human cost of being able to meet it.
03 — pages

The fastest improvement is often reducing what pages

The chapter tells teams with extreme pager volume to first ask whether every current page should be a page. Some conditions should be repaired automatically. Some should create a ticket. Some are simply diagnostic logging.

A page must represent urgent, important, actionable work. If the responder repeatedly acknowledges an alert and takes no action, the monitoring system is asking a human to perform classification that software should have performed.

Grouping related alerts is also essential. One incident should not fan out into a dozen independent pages just because multiple symptoms are visible.

Pager-budget example:
A team’s target is 2 incidents/shift but observes 5.
Excess = 3 incidents/shift = 150% above the budget.
If a shift has 12 hours, even 1.5h average effort per incident means 7.5h of reactive incident work before tickets and handoff.
04 — knowledge

Prevent knowledge gaps and hero dependence

High turnover or a growing service portfolio creates a knowledge problem. Build architecture documentation, runbooks, escalation notes, and shadowing into the rotation rather than relying on one expert’s memory.

On-call training should include actual troubleshooting. A runbook that lists commands without explaining service behavior creates brittle responders who cannot adapt when the failure differs slightly from the documented path.

Blameless reviews matter for psychological safety. Engineers who fear being judged for escalation or uncertainty will take riskier actions alone.

05 — data

Use paging data to decide which bugs to fix

Once alerts are linked to bugs/root causes, the team can answer objective questions: which component causes the most pages, which manual action is repeated most often, which alerts remain unroot-caused, and which issue is truly worst rather than merely memorable.

The chapter recommends collecting this data systematically rather than maintaining an ad hoc spreadsheet. Linking paging events to bugs allows the issue tracker to become a reliability backlog with evidence.

This supports prioritization by frequency and impact. Fixing the most dramatic one-off alert may save less human time than eliminating a less severe page that fires every week.

Illustrative prioritization:
Bug A: 1 page/month × 4h follow-up = 4h/month.
Bug B: 8 pages/month × 45 min = 6h/month.
Bug B looks smaller per incident but costs more recurring on-call time.
06 — health

On-call should produce engineering work

A sustainable system closes the loop: repeated pages become bug fixes, automation, better SLO-based alerting, or architectural work.

If a service consistently exceeds what the team can support, the answer is not indefinite heroics. Reduce responsibility, change the SLO, involve developers, or fix the service before expanding the rotation.

Conversely, an extremely quiet rotation can create operational underload. If engineers rarely encounter production, drills and deliberate training are needed so knowledge does not atrophy.

Anti-pattern: celebrate people who survive a hundred-page shift instead of fixing why the monitoring and service generated a hundred pages.
