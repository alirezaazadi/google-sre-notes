Eliminating Toil

Toil is the recurring operational work that grows with the service but produces little lasting value. This chapter moves beyond the definition: it shows how to identify and measure toil, compare remediation projects, and redesign systems and processes so the work disappears rather than merely becoming easier to perform.

01 — definition

What the Workbook means by toil

The chapter treats toil as a spectrum. A task becomes more toil-like when it is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, lacks enduring value, and grows in direct proportion to service size.

The running simple example is a full temporary directory. An engineer logs in, finds files, and deletes them. The action is manual and repetitive; a program could perform it; the result restores service but leaves no durable improvement; and as the fleet grows, the number of such incidents tends to grow too.

Operational work is not automatically toil. Investigating a novel failure can create deep system knowledge and may directly influence design. A one-time migration may be operational but not repetitive. The useful question is whether the work keeps coming back without improving the system.

CharacteristicQuestion to ask
ManualDoes a human have to execute the steps?
RepetitiveWill essentially the same work return?
AutomatableCould software make the decision or execute the action?
TacticalDoes it solve the immediate symptom rather than improve the system?
No enduring valueAfter completion, is the system no easier to operate next time?
Scales with serviceDoes growth create proportionally more of the same work?
02 — why

Why toil needs an explicit limit

The chapter reiterates Google’s 50% upper bound on total operational work. The exact percentage is not the universal lesson; the important practice is setting a limit so production maintenance cannot consume all engineering capacity.

Toil creates a vicious cycle. More manual work reduces time for automation and redesign; lack of redesign makes the next month even more manual. This is one reason a healthy SRE team needs protected project time.

Toil also has less obvious costs: context switching, burnout, slower incident response, inconsistent execution, increased human-error risk, and difficulty onboarding new engineers.

Illustrative toil accounting:
8 engineers × 40h/week = 320 engineer-hours.
If pages, repetitive tickets, and manual changes consume 176h, operational work = 176 ÷ 320 = 55%.
Even if every ticket is individually justified, the team has crossed a sustainability boundary.
03 — measurement

Identify and measure before choosing a solution

The chapter strongly advocates a data-driven approach. Record the recurring activity, its frequency, the human time required, and the expected future growth. Google SRE teams often track toil in bugs and compare the cost of fixing the source with the time the fix would save.

Use units that reflect real human effort. Ticket count alone may be misleading if one ticket takes three minutes and another takes six hours. Time, number of manual interventions, or a weighted cost can be more informative.

Include context-switch cost. Ten five-minute interrupts do not necessarily cost only fifty minutes because each interruption breaks concentration and delays the return to project work.

Measure again after the project. An automation that moves the human step somewhere else is not a successful toil reduction.

ROI framing: toil reduction is an investment. Estimate both development/maintenance cost and saved human time, but also include risk reduction and quality improvements.
04 — strategy

Prefer eliminating the source over automating the symptom

The best outcome is often changing the system so the operational task is no longer needed. Before writing automation, ask why the task exists, whether the requirement can disappear, and whether users can self-service.

If the task remains necessary, simplify the workflow first. Automation built on a confusing manual procedure tends to encode every historical special case.

The chapter’s examples show several levels of improvement: automated detection, automated draining, automated repair attempts, automatic verification, and only involving a technician when physical intervention is truly required. The design goal is to reduce the number of human decision points.

Standardization is itself a toil-reduction mechanism. A uniform repair path or a common service interface lets one piece of automation handle many cases.

Detect condition
Choose standard action
Drain / isolate
Repair automatically
Verify
Escalate only if needed
05 — case-study

Network repair automation: from procedure to closed loop

In the Jupiter network case study, the team replaced a technician-heavy repair procedure with a more uniform automated workflow. When a switch problem was detected, the repair system could drain the switch, record the reason, attempt power-cycle repairs, verify success, and undrain it.

A useful detail is the policy encoded in the automation. If the same failure recurred within a defined interval, the system could stop repeating the cheap repair and escalate to component replacement. Automation therefore did not merely copy commands; it encoded operational judgment and history.

This is the difference between a script and an operational system. The system knows the desired safe state, tracks prior outcomes, and decides when a human is required.

Failure mode: automate every keystroke but leave all diagnosis and decision-making with a person. That reduces typing but may not reduce interrupts.
06 — decommission

Sometimes the correct toil project is deleting a system

The chapter’s decommissioning example deals with legacy home directories spread across a very large footprint: billions of files, hundreds of terabytes, many users, volumes, appliances, and geographic sites.

Manual ticket-by-ticket migration was not scalable. The project first built data collection to understand real user workflows, then created low-touch self-service tooling for communication, archiving, and migration.

The deeper lesson is important: decommissioning can be reliability engineering. Removing an obsolete platform eliminates its entire future operational cost, security burden, maintenance, and pager surface.

Large toil projects therefore need product thinking: understand users, communicate change, design migration paths, and measure progress. The users of an SRE automation are still users.

Source scale:
The decommissioning analysis covered about 2.5 billion files, 300 TB of disk, 60,000 users, 400 volumes, 124 NAS appliances, and 60 sites.
A manual ticket process at that scale is not merely inconvenient—it is structurally impossible.
