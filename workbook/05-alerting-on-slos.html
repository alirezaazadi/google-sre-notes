Alerting on SLOs

SLO alerting is a signal-processing problem: detect incidents that threaten a meaningful fraction of the error budget with good precision and recall, while keeping detection and reset times short. The chapter arrives at multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts by showing why simpler rules fail.

01 — criteria

Four properties define a good SLO alert

The alert should fire for significant events—events that consume enough error budget to require intervention. The chapter evaluates strategies using four dimensions.

Precision asks what fraction of alerts correspond to significant events. Low precision means pager noise. Recall asks what fraction of significant events are detected. Low recall means real incidents are missed. Detection time measures how quickly a harmful event pages. Reset time measures how quickly the alert stops after recovery.

No single threshold is automatically good along all four dimensions. The rest of the chapter is a progression of increasingly better compromises.

PropertyBad outcome when weak
PrecisionToo many insignificant events page humans.
RecallImportant SLO-threatening incidents are invisible.
Detection timeA fast-burning outage spends too much budget before response begins.
Reset timeThe page remains active after recovery, causing confusion and alert fatigue.
02 — naive

Why alerting directly on the SLO threshold is noisy

Suppose a 99.9% availability SLO allows a 0.1% error rate. A rule that pages whenever a one-minute error rate exceeds 0.1% reacts quickly, but it treats a tiny transient burst as if it were a serious monthly reliability event.

Lengthening the window improves precision because the bad rate must persist longer. But the chapter shows a major downside: a long averaging window has a long memory. A severe outage can recover while the long-window average remains above threshold for many hours.

Adding a fixed for: 1h duration is also weak. A 100% outage and a barely-bad 0.11% error rate both wait for the same duration; brief recovery resets the timer, so repeated severe spikes can escape the rule.

Key failure: threshold + duration measures 'how long was the metric above a line?', not 'how much reliability budget did this event consume?'.
03 — burn-rate

Burn rate normalizes incident severity

Burn rate asks how fast you are consuming the error budget relative to the sustainable rate. A burn rate of 1 means the service would consume the entire budget exactly over the SLO window. A burn rate of 10 means it is spending reliability margin ten times too quickly.

This representation lets you use the same reasoning across SLO targets. A 14.4× burn rate has the same interpretation—rapid budget depletion—whether the service target is 99%, 99.9%, or 99.99%.

Different burn rates can map to different operational responses. A catastrophic fast burn should page quickly; a slow but sustained budget leak may be appropriate for a ticket.

Worked example:
SLO = 99.9% → allowed error rate = 0.1%.
Observed errors = 2% over the alert window.
Burn rate = 2% ÷ 0.1% = 20×.
If that rate continued, a 30-day error budget would last roughly 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5 days.
04 — multiple

Use multiple burn-rate tiers

One burn rate cannot simultaneously detect a complete outage quickly and a small chronic problem efficiently. The chapter therefore introduces multiple burn-rate windows.

High burn rates use shorter windows and urgent pages. Lower burn rates use longer windows and less urgent notification. This design approximates the intuition that a huge outage deserves response after minutes, while a small but steady error leak must persist long enough to prove it matters.

A side effect is overlapping alerts: a very severe event may satisfy several slower conditions as well. Alert suppression or routing logic should prevent one incident from producing multiple redundant notifications.

PatternPurpose
Fast burn + shorter windowCatch catastrophic incidents before they consume the budget.
Medium burnCatch serious sustained degradation.
Slow burn + long windowCatch chronic reliability loss without paging on noise.
05 — multiwindow

Why multiwindow, multi-burn-rate is the recommended pattern

The final refinement adds a short confirmation window to each long burn-rate window. The long window proves the event has consumed enough budget to matter; the short window verifies the service is still burning at that rate.

The chapter gives a useful guideline: make the short window around one-twelfth the long window. The long condition provides precision; the short condition gives fast reset after recovery.

This architecture avoids the stale-alert problem of a single long window: once the recent short window becomes healthy, the alert can clear even if the long historical average is still elevated.

Memory rule: long window = significance; short window = still happening.
06 — edge-cases

Low traffic and extreme SLOs need special handling

At very low traffic, one or two failed requests can dominate the percentage, so purely ratio-based alerting can become unstable. The alerting design may need absolute event thresholds or other safeguards.

Very loose SLOs can make standard paging thresholds impossible to reach. The chapter notes that with a 90% SLO, a complete one-hour outage may consume less than a common 2%-of-budget paging threshold for a long SLO window.

At the opposite extreme, a 99.999% monthly SLO has such a tiny budget that a 100% outage can exhaust it in about 26 seconds—less than the collection and notification latency of many monitoring systems. The SLO and alerting architecture must therefore be physically achievable.

Do not copy burn-rate numbers blindly. The design must reflect the service’s traffic, collection interval, SLO window, and operational response capability.

Source edge case:
99.999% monthly availability → 0.001% budget.
30 days × 24 × 60 × 60 ≈ 2,592,000 seconds.
0.00001 × 2,592,000 ≈ 25.9 seconds of complete outage budget.
