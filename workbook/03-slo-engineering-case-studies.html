01 — why case studies

Why these stories matter

The chapter is intentionally practical. It shows that SLO adoption is not a one-time technical configuration task. It changes how companies talk to cloud providers, how developers take operational responsibility, how leadership sees service health, and how reliability work gets prioritized.

Both organizations begin from existing operational pain and imperfect observability. Neither waits until every user interaction has a mathematically perfect SLI. They establish a useful common language and improve it as the organization learns.

Lesson: a useful SLO in active decision-making is more valuable than a theoretically perfect SLO that never ships.
02 — evernote

Evernote: reliability through the customer’s lens

Evernote entered the story with substantial scale: more than 220 million users, over 12 billion stored pieces of information, and more than 750 MySQL instances. Moving a service of that size to cloud infrastructure made provider/customer reliability alignment important.

Evernote did not want a hard boundary where Google only looked at global cloud metrics while Evernote dealt with application impact. A provider can remain above its published global SLO while an outage concentrated in Evernote’s region materially harms Evernote users.

The company therefore shared its own SLO view with Google CRE. The objective was to align both organizations on the performance characteristics that mattered to Evernote, share success and failure, and investigate incidents using Evernote’s user-visible perspective rather than only infrastructure aggregates.

Evernote’s SLO process also included recurring reviews. Reviews connected actual outages to SLO misses and created follow-up work. As the measures matured, the cadence and definitions could be refined.

Why provider aggregation can hide pain:
If a provider operates 100,000 VMs and 200 Evernote VMs in one region fail, only 0.2% of the hypothetical global VM fleet is affected. Evernote, however, may lose a large fraction of its own regional capacity. The same event has very different denominators.
03 — home-depot

The Home Depot: from ticket-centric operations to VALET

The Home Depot’s story starts from a different organizational model. Developers increasingly became responsible for operating the software they built, so they needed service-level measures that communicated reliability both to internal consumers and product management.

The organization created the mnemonic VALET: Volume, Availability, Latency, Errors, Tickets. The first four resemble familiar serving-system signals; Tickets deliberately captures a human-operability dimension inherited from the company’s earlier way of evaluating production software.

Volume asks how much business demand the service can handle. Availability asks whether it is reachable when needed. Latency asks whether it responds fast enough. Errors asks whether operations fail. Tickets asks whether otherwise-successful operation still depends on recurring human intervention.

The point of VALET is not that every organization should use exactly these five metrics. Its value is that the mnemonic made the SLO conversation understandable to a large enterprise.

VALETQuestion
VolumeHow much business traffic can this service process?
AvailabilityIs it usable when consumers need it?
LatencyIs the response fast enough for its use case?
ErrorsDoes the request fail because of the service?
TicketsDoes routine operation still require manual intervention?
04 — evangelism

SLO adoption is an education problem

The Home Depot did not assume that engineers and leaders already understood SLOs, SLAs, and error budgets. The team deliberately explained why SLOs mattered, what to measure, how SLOs fit a 'freedom and responsibility' culture, and what teams should do with the results.

Weekly reporting, workshops, internal talks, and visible leadership support helped create familiarity. This matters because an SLO is only useful if product owners, developers, and operators interpret it similarly.

Once the process had demonstrated value, the organization automated collection and reporting. This sequencing is important: automate a useful decision process, not a dashboard that nobody trusts.

Anti-pattern: central reliability team creates SLO dashboards for every service and then expects product teams to care. Adoption requires shared ownership of the meaning and consequences.
05 — comparison

What both companies teach

The Evernote story emphasizes aligning provider reliability with the reliability experienced by one customer. The Home Depot story emphasizes scaling a reliability vocabulary across a large developer organization.

Both demonstrate that SLOs are boundary objects: they let teams with different roles discuss the same service outcome using objective data.

Both also show that the first SLO is part of a journey. Real incidents and reviews reveal which measurements are missing, which objectives are too strict or too loose, and which organizational processes need to change.

Questions you should be able to explain aloud

These are not trivia questions. A strong answer explains the mechanism, the reason for it, and the trade-off.

Question 1

Why can a cloud provider be within SLO while your application has a serious outage?

Question 2

What is VALET, and why does the Tickets dimension exist?

Question 3

If a company has no SLO culture, what would you do in the first 90 days?

Question 4

Why should automation come after teams understand the SLO process?
