Incident Response

Incident response separates two simultaneous problems: restoring the service and coordinating the people restoring it. The chapter adapts Incident Command System ideas into Google/PagerDuty practice, emphasizing early declaration, explicit roles, centralized communication, generic mitigation, and mitigation before root-cause perfection.

01 — structure

Why incident management exists

An outage involving several people or teams creates a second failure mode: coordination itself can break. Engineers duplicate work, apply conflicting changes, repeatedly explain the situation to new arrivals, or fail to communicate externally.

The chapter distinguishes resolving an incident—mitigating impact and restoring service—from managing an incident—coordinating responders and information flow.

Google’s approach derives from Incident Command System practices. The framework is useful because teams agree on roles and communication before the emergency, leaving more cognitive capacity for the technical problem.

The 3 Cs: coordinate the response, communicate clearly, and maintain control.
02 — roles

Incident Commander, Operations Lead, Communications Lead

The Incident Commander (IC) owns the high-level response. The IC coordinates work, delegates roles, keeps the response controlled, and ensures communication happens. The IC is not required to be the most technically knowledgeable person or the person typing commands.

The Operations Lead directs technical mitigation and investigation. Multiple technical task forces can report through this role.

The Communications Lead maintains updates for responders and stakeholders and absorbs requests for status that would otherwise interrupt technical responders.

Roles can expand, contract, or be handed off as the incident changes. Explicit handoff matters because ambiguity over who is in command is itself operational risk.

RolePrimary jobShould avoid
Incident CommanderPriorities, coordination, overall controlBeing trapped in one debugging thread.
Operations LeadTechnical mitigation and investigationsOwning all communication.
Communications LeadInternal/external status and inquiriesMaking uncoordinated technical changes.
03 — home

Case study: Google Home and the cost of not declaring early

A Google Assistant release caused speaker-recognition data to be fetched far more often than intended—roughly every 30 minutes instead of once per day. Quota increases temporarily reduced symptoms, but the release continued even though the real cause had not been identified.

As rollout reached more users, errors returned. The problem crossed a weekend while developers were not readily available, and customer reports grew. The team still treated the issue as ordinary bug-tracker troubleshooting rather than a managed incident.

This produced fragmented communication between client and server developers and repeated mitigations without a coordinated decision to stop rollout until the cause was understood.

The review concludes that early incident declaration would have centralized communication, brought relevant teams together sooner, and likely shortened the event.

Traffic amplification:
Expected fetch ≈ 1/day.
Actual fetch ≈ every 30 min = 48/day.
That is roughly a 48× increase in this activity before considering user growth or retry effects.
Operational lesson: a mitigation that makes graphs green is not proof that the underlying incident is over.
04 — gke

Case study: GKE and the value of formal structure

A GKE incident prevented new clusters from being created in Europe. The initial on-call quickly verified user impact and declared an incident, but the response grew to many teams and specialists. At first, investigations still happened somewhat independently.

When a more experienced responder took incident command, the team formalized roles: IC, Ops Lead, Communications Lead, external communications, and multiple task forces. A shared working document became the place for detailed debugging while the chat channel became the decision hub.

The apparent clue was a corrupted container image, but the deeper issue involved a cached copy in Google Container Registry. This is a useful reminder that the most visible upstream dependency can be a distraction.

The outage lasted 6 hours and 40 minutes, involved 41 people appearing in the incident channel, six on-call teams plus additional specialists, and produced 28 action items. Structure was necessary because no single engineer could hold the complete system in their head.

Scaling rule: as the number of responders grows, coordination overhead grows faster than linearly unless the work is partitioned and information is centralized.
05 — mitigation

Mitigate first; root cause second

The chapter explicitly orders active incident work: assess impact, mitigate impact, perform root-cause analysis, then after recovery fix the underlying problem and write the postmortem.

Generic mitigations are prebuilt blunt tools that reduce user pain without requiring a precise diagnosis: rollback a recent release, drain a region, disable a feature, fail over, or add safe capacity. They can have side effects, but they are valuable because they are available early.

In the GKE case, a generic rollback to a known-good image path could have reduced user impact before the exact cache behavior was fully understood. The right time to build those tools is before the emergency.

Customers care first that errors stop. SRE should not keep users in an outage merely because engineers want intellectual certainty.

06 — prepared

What a mature incident process looks like

Declare early and downgrade later if necessary. Use one communication hub and one shared state document. Assign owners to independent work streams. Keep a timeline of observations, decisions, mitigations, and results.

Perform risky rollouts during times when the people needed to respond are actually available, or provide explicit on-call coverage.

After the incident, build the generic mitigations and diagnostics that would have shortened it, then exercise them in drills. Incident response is therefore not only a process; it is a source of engineering requirements.

Anti-pattern: dozens of experts join a chat, each investigates a plausible cause, and nobody owns the overall decision about what to do next.
