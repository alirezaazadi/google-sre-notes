SRE Engagement Model

SRE is a scarce engineering resource, so the relationship between SRE and product development must be deliberate. An engagement defines the reliability problem, shared objectives, ownership, production-readiness expectations, and when the relationship should change or end.

01 — scarcity

Not every service needs dedicated SRE

Google intentionally cannot staff every service with dedicated SRE. The scarcity creates a useful forcing function: SRE effort should go where specialized reliability engineering can produce meaningful benefit.

A service can adopt SRE principles—SLOs, error budgets, production readiness, automation, postmortems—without an SRE team carrying its pager. Dedicated operational support is one possible engagement, not the definition of SRE.

02 — shared goals

Align feature velocity and long-term viability

Product teams and SRE generally agree that availability, performance, scalability, efficiency, and development velocity matter. Their local pressures differ: a product team may face a launch date while SRE sees accumulated reliability risk.

An explicit SLO/error budget gives the relationship a shared metric. SRE can support rapid release while budget is healthy; development can commit engineering effort to reliability when the service is consuming too much budget.

The relationship should preserve product-team ownership. Application developers should own their changes and participate in resolving production defects rather than externalizing all operational cost.

03 — readiness

Make production readiness explicit

Before an ongoing operational relationship, review architecture, dependencies, monitoring, emergency response, capacity, change management, performance, and operational procedures. The exact mechanism may be a Production Readiness Review or a more continuous readiness process.

Avoid one-off manual scripts as a substitute for a supportable operating model. If a service requires special operator memory for routine changes, that is a readiness gap.

04 — scale

Design engagement for leverage

Because SRE staffing is a small fraction of overall engineering—Google describes SRE as less than roughly 10% of engineering in the chapter—one SRE team often needs to support several similar services or create common infrastructure rather than become custom operators for each product.

Grouping services with similar operational patterns increases leverage: one automation, dashboard framework, or release mechanism can benefit many teams.

05 — lifecycle

Engagements should have an outcome

Consulting may solve an architectural or SLO problem without pager ownership. A project/embedded engagement may prepare a large migration or launch. Ongoing support can make sense for a critical service whose operational complexity justifies it.

Measure whether the engagement reduces user risk, toil, operational load, or launch risk. If the service becomes mature and self-sufficient, SRE should be able to move effort to a higher-leverage problem.

06 — lifecycle

The right SRE contribution changes with the service lifecycle

During architecture/design, SRE’s highest leverage is preventing expensive mistakes: resilience guidance, infrastructure constraints, prototypes, and design review. During active development, the work shifts toward productionization—capacity, redundancy, overload behavior, monitoring, alerting, and performance.

Before general availability, SRE should help define SLOs, build capacity models, prepare launch resources, and share operational work with developers so both sides learn the service’s real failure modes. After GA, SRE may carry more operations, but developers should retain enough production contact that reliability problems remain visible in product priorities.

Deprecation and abandonment are also part of the lifecycle. SRE may temporarily support both an old and replacement service during migration, then hand remaining operations back or remove the service completely. Engagement is therefore not synonymous with permanent pager ownership.

07 — scarce resource

SRE engagement should produce measurable leverage

The chapter notes that SRE capacity is intentionally scarce relative to development—Google generally operates with an SRE-to-developer ratio below 10%. One SRE team may therefore need to support several related services or choose where direct support has the highest value.

Shared goals make this trade-off explicit. SRE can commit to enabling releases as quickly as is safe; development can commit engineering time to structural reliability problems and technical debt. SLO/error-budget state becomes the evidence used to adjust priorities.

An engagement should define scope, milestones, an end-state success story, feedback cadence, and a way to measure maturity before and after. One-off scripts that help only the partner team are weaker than improvements to common tools and automation that benefit the whole company.

Scarcity cue from the chapter: Google generally maintains an SRE-to-developer ratio of <10%; dedicated SRE support therefore cannot scale one-for-one with services.
