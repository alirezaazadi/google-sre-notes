Appendix B — Example Error Budget Policy

An error-budget policy specifies what teams do when measured reliability falls below the agreed objective. The example is intentionally not a punishment system; it is a pre-agreed mechanism for shifting engineering priority toward reliability when continued change would impose too much user risk.

01 — goals

Turn reliability arguments into policy

The policy protects customers from repeated SLO misses, creates an objective incentive to balance features with reliability, and gives teams a pre-agreed way to resolve the common argument “should we launch or fix reliability?”

The appendix explicitly states a non-goal: punishing teams. Freezing some change is itself undesirable because the business loses feature velocity. It is justified only when reliability evidence says the service is already consuming more risk than agreed.

02 — behavior

What happens when the budget is exhausted

When the service is within SLO, releases and data/config changes proceed under the normal release policy. When it has exceeded its budget over the specified rolling window, the example policy halts nonessential changes until reliability is back within the objective.

Emergency/security fixes remain possible. The point is not to freeze the service into a broken state; the point is to stop adding unrelated risk while engineering attention is focused on returning the service to the agreed level.

03 — responsibility

Cause affects the corrective work

If service code, a procedure, or an avoidable dependency design consumed the budget, the relevant teams should devote effort to correcting that failure class. A postmortem may show that a dependency assumed to be hard/reliable should instead be softened, cached, replicated, or handled with graceful degradation.

The policy also needs an authoritative data source and an escalation mechanism for disagreements. “SRE feels unsafe” is not a sufficiently objective release policy.

04 — worked example

Translate the SLO into a decision

Suppose a service has a 99.9% request-success SLO over a four-week window and receives 50,000,000 valid requests.
budget = 0.1% × 50,000,000 = 50,000 bad requests.
If incidents produce 58,000 bad requests, the budget is exhausted; the pre-agreed policy—not an improvised argument—defines the change restrictions and reliability work.
Operational value: write the policy while everyone is calm, before a high-profile launch competes with an active reliability deficit.

Questions you should be able to explain aloud
