definition

Toil isn't a feeling — it's six specific traits

The opening move of the chapter is a deliberate narrowing: toil isn't "grungy work," isn't "chores I don't enjoy," and isn't the same as overhead (necessary admin like team meetings, HR paperwork, performance reviews — real, but not what this chapter is about). It's a specific category of operational work, and the more of these six traits a task has, the more it counts as toil:

sketch — the six traits of toil (original diagram)

Two of these deserve a second look because they're the ones people misjudge most:

  • "Automatable" isn't the same as "currently unautomated." The bar is whether a machine could do it, or whether the need for it could be designed away entirely — not whether someone has gotten around to writing the script yet. There's a subtlety the chapter flags explicitly: a task isn't automatically "not toil" just because it needs human judgment right now. If a poorly designed system pages someone several times a day, each requiring judgment, that's still toil — it's a signal the system needs a redesign, not proof that a human must stay in the loop forever.
  • "No enduring value" is the sharpest test. If the service is in the same state after the task as before it, that's toil, even if the task itself was tedious and skill-intensive. Cleaning up a service's entire alerting config, removing years of clutter — grungy, yes, but it leaves the system permanently better, so it's not toil.
the budget

Why toil gets a hard ceiling, and where the floor comes from

This is the same 50% cap introduced in Chapter 1, revisited with the actual arithmetic behind it. Toil expands to fill all available time if nobody bounds it — so Google caps it explicitly, and treats persistent overshoot as an organizational bug, not a personal failing.

6-person on-call rotation → 1 week primary + 1 week secondary per 6-week cycle
→ floor of 2/6 ≈ 33% toil, just from being on the rotation

8-person rotation → 2/8 = 25% floor

Actual measured average across Google SREs (quarterly survey): ~33%

Notice what this floor implies: even a perfectly-run team can't get toil below roughly 2/rotation size, just from on-call coverage alone. Bigger rotations genuinely buy you a lower toil floor — one concrete reason team-sizing itself is a toil-reduction lever, not just a staffing decision.

The survey data also flags where toil actually comes from, in order: interrupts (non-urgent pings and emails) rank highest, then on-call/urgent response, then releases and pushes — notable because releases are usually already automated to some degree, meaning there's still real room to push further there.

Averages hide outliers. A team-wide 33% average can coexist with individuals at 0% (pure project work) and others at 80% (buried). A lopsided distribution like that is itself the signal — it usually means toil needs to be redistributed across the team, not that the average is "fine."
the opposite of toil

What actually counts as engineering

If toil is manual, repetitive, and leaves no lasting trace, engineering is its mirror image: novel, judgment-driven, and it permanently changes the system for the better. The chapter splits real SRE activity into four buckets:

CategoryWhat it looks like
Software engineeringWriting/modifying code — automation scripts, tools, frameworks, reliability/scalability features
Systems engineeringConfiguring production systems in ways that produce a lasting improvement from a one-time effort — monitoring setup, load balancer config, architecture consulting
ToilRepetitive, manual, tied to running the service, no lasting improvement
OverheadAdmin not tied to running the service — hiring, meetings, self-assessments, training

The target is that engineering work (the first two rows) should average at least 50% of an SRE's time — averaged over a quarter or a year, because toil is naturally spiky (an incident-heavy week can blow past the target temporarily without being a problem). What matters is the long-run trend: if a team is consistently well under 50% engineering time, that's the signal something structural needs fixing.

nuance

Toil in small doses isn't the enemy

The chapter is careful not to moralize toil out of existence entirely. Small, predictable, repetitive tasks can genuinely be calming — quick wins, low stress, low risk — and some people actively prefer them. A little toil is fine, and completely normal in any engineering role, including this one.

The problem is scale, not existence. Toil becomes toxic in large quantities, for reasons that compound on each other:
  • Career stagnation — you can't build a promotion case out of grunt work alone, however impactful any one instance of it was.
  • Low morale → burnout — everyone has a personal toil ceiling, even people who don't mind small amounts.
  • Organizational confusion — SRE's whole pitch to new hires is "engineering org, not an ops shop"; a team drowning in toil quietly breaks that promise.
  • Slower feature velocity — a team buried in manual work can't ship the automation or features that would have reduced next quarter's toil.
  • Bad incentives set for other teams — being too willing to absorb toil teaches product-dev counterparts that they can keep offloading operational work onto SRE instead of fixing root causes themselves.
  • Attrition — even engineers personally tolerant of toil will watch their best teammates leave for more rewarding work elsewhere if the team's toil load stays high.
