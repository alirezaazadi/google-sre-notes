the shape of a shift

Response time is a direct consequence of your SLO, not a separate policy

Being on-call means being reachable and able to act on production within an agreed response time — typically 5 minutes for user-facing, highly time-critical services, and up to 30 minutes for less urgent ones. This number isn't arbitrary; it falls directly out of the error budget math from Chapter 3–4:

99.99% quarterly availability target → ~13 minutes of allowed downtime, total, for the whole quarter
→ on-call response time has to be on the order of minutes, or a single incident could burn the entire budget

Most teams run both a primary and secondary on-call rotation simultaneously. How duties split between them varies — sometimes secondary is a pure fallback for missed pages, sometimes primary handles only pages while secondary absorbs all the non-urgent production work. Some team pairs even serve as each other's secondary, avoiding the need for a dedicated secondary rotation altogether.

balance: quantity

The arithmetic behind minimum team size

This is the chapter's most directly reusable piece of math. Starting from the 50% engineering-time floor established in Chapter 5, the on-call-specific constraint is stricter: at most 25% of an SRE's time on-call, leaving the other 25% for non-paging operational work (tickets, releases).

sketch — the time budget behind the minimum rotation size (original diagram)

Working backward from the 25% cap, with two people on-call at all times (primary + secondary), and week-long shifts:

Single-site team: minimum 8 engineers
→ each engineer on-call (primary or secondary) 1 week out of every 4
→ 1/4 = 25%, exactly at the ceiling

Dual-site ("follow the sun") team: minimum 6 engineers per site

Why go multi-site at all, given the extra coordination overhead it costs? Two concrete reasons: night shifts have documented negative health effects, and a "follow the sun" rotation across time zones can eliminate them entirely; and — separately — keeping the rotation from growing too large (discussed below) keeps every engineer regularly in contact with production. Multi-site isn't free, though — it trades operational health for real communication and coordination costs, so it's a genuine tradeoff to weigh against a system's actual importance and workload, not an automatic upgrade.

balance: quality

Not just how much on-call — how rough is each shift

Team size alone doesn't capture whether on-call is sustainable — a small rotation with a quiet system can be fine, while a large rotation on a chronically noisy one still burns people out. The chapter defines an incident as a related sequence of events/alerts that would all show up in the same postmortem, and anchors the quality bound in measured reality: root-cause analysis, remediation, and postmortem follow-up together average about 6 hours of work per incident.

6 hours per incident → max ~2 incidents per 12-hour on-call shift
target distribution: flat over time, median close to 0 incidents/day

The "median near 0" detail matters more than it might look: if a specific component pages every single day (median > 1), that's not "handled" just because each individual incident stays under the 2-per-shift ceiling — it's a strong signal that something else is quietly deteriorating in parallel, and the real per-shift load is likely to spike above the target once that second thing breaks too.

compensation

Paying for on-call is itself a load-limiting mechanism

Google compensates on-call with either time-off-in-lieu or direct pay, capped at some proportion of overall salary. The cap isn't incidental — it functions as a built-in ceiling on how much on-call load any one person can be incentivized to absorb, which protects against the perverse outcome where compensation alone would otherwise encourage someone to volunteer for unsustainable amounts of on-call work.

the psychology

Why "trust your gut" is bad advice mid-incident

This section brings in real cognitive-science grounding, and it's one of the more distinctive parts of the chapter. Human decision-making broadly runs on two different systems: fast, intuitive, automatic reactions, versus slower, deliberate, rational analysis. For a complex distributed-systems outage, the deliberate mode reliably produces better outcomes — but stress actively works against it.

Stress hormones (cortisol, CRH) measurably impair the cognitive functions the deliberate mode depends on, pushing behavior toward unreflective, habitual action — which is exactly when heuristics and confirmation bias become most tempting. The chapter's own example: an alert pages for the fourth time this week, and the first three were caused by the same known external issue — it's extremely tempting to assume cause #4 is "obviously" the same thing again, without actually checking.

The core warning: intuition and quick reactions feel reassuring mid-incident, but intuition is frequently wrong and hard to justify with actual data — and pursuing a plausible-but-incorrect hunch from the very start wastes exactly the time an incident can least afford to lose.

What actually reduces the stress, structurally

The chapter doesn't just diagnose the problem — it names three concrete organizational supports that lower the felt stakes of any single decision an on-call engineer has to make alone:

  • Clear escalation paths — the developer teams behind SRE-supported systems typically run their own 24/7 rotation too, so escalating a genuinely unfamiliar or ambiguous problem is always a legitimate, expected option, not an admission of failure.
  • A formal incident-management protocol (detailed in Ch.14) for anything spanning multiple teams or lacking a clear time bound — offloading the cognitive overhead of "who does what, how do we communicate status" onto a pre-defined structure, often backed by tooling that automates the rote parts (formatting updates, handing off roles) so the engineer's attention stays on the actual problem.
  • A blameless postmortem culture — knowing that a wrong call made under pressure will be examined as a systems question, not a personal one, removes a significant source of decision-paralysis in the moment.
too much load

Operational overload — and what to actually do about it

When on-call load creeps past the 25% ceiling, the fix is treated as a real, trackable objective for the quarter — not a vague aspiration. The chapter suggests making the symptoms explicitly measurable (e.g., "daily tickets < 5," "paging events per shift < 2") so progress is verifiable rather than a matter of impression.

Two specific, common root causes get called out directly:

  • Misconfigured alerting — paging rules not actually tied to real SLO threats, or alerts that aren't genuinely actionable. This is Chapter 6's paging philosophy showing up again as a lived operational cost, not just a design principle.
  • Alert fan-out — a single underlying condition triggering many separate alerts. The fix is grouping related alerts at the monitoring layer so the ratio approaches 1 alert per 1 incident — otherwise the on-call engineer burns real time triaging duplicates instead of addressing the actual problem.

When the root cause is outside SRE's own control — say, application developers shipping changes that make the system noisier or less reliable — the chapter's escalation path is notably blunt: SRE can "give back the pager," routing on-call responsibility back to the developer team until the system meets SRE's bar. This is explicitly framed as rare and usually temporary — most of the time, working jointly with developers to reduce load is enough — but the option existing at all is itself meaningful. It's a structural check that keeps the reliability-vs-velocity tension (from Chapter 1's error budget discussion) actually balanced in practice, rather than being a one-directional obligation SRE always has to absorb.

the opposite failure

Operational underload: a quiet system is its own risk

This is the chapter's least intuitive section, and worth sitting with. Too little on-call exposure sounds like a good problem to have — but going too long without touching production creates real knowledge gaps and confidence distortions (in either direction — overconfidence or underconfidence), and those gaps only surface at the worst possible moment: during an actual incident.

The chapter's fix: deliberately size rotations so every engineer is on-call at least once or twice a quarter, and supplement real incidents with practice — "Wheel of Misfortune" tabletop exercises (simulated incidents for training, covered in Ch.28) and Google's company-wide DiRT (Disaster Recovery Training) drills, which combine theoretical and hands-on multi-day testing of real infrastructure and services.

The throughline connecting overload and underload: both are really the same underlying problem — a mismatch between an engineer's actual, current familiarity with production and what an incident will demand of them under pressure. Overload wears that familiarity down through sheer volume; underload lets it silently atrophy from disuse. The fix in both directions is the same instinct: keep exposure to real production work in a sustainable, bounded range — neither flooded nor starved.
