Chapter 5: Alerting on SLOs

Six increasingly clever ways to turn "we're burning our error budget" into a real alert — and why multiwindow, multi-burn-rate is the one Google actually recommends.

the four dials

Precision, recall, detection time, reset time

Turning an SLO into an actual alert means finding a rule that balances four properties simultaneously — and, as this chapter shows in genuinely satisfying step-by-step detail, most naive attempts optimize one at the expense of the others:

PropertyDefinition
PrecisionOf the alerts that fired, what fraction corresponded to a genuinely significant event?
RecallOf the genuinely significant events, what fraction actually triggered an alert?
Detection timeHow long between a real problem starting and a notification going out?
Reset timeHow long does the alert keep firing after the underlying issue is actually resolved?

The chapter walks through six approaches in increasing order of sophistication — each one a genuine, working attempt that fixes the previous approach's specific flaw, while (often) introducing a new one. This progression is worth following in full, because each failure mode is a real, common mistake teams make in practice.

attempt 1

Alert when the instantaneous error rate exceeds the SLO

expr: job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate10m{job="myjob"} >= 0.001 (99.9% SLO → alert if the last 10 minutes' error rate ≥ 0.1%)

Detection time: excellent — 0.6 seconds for a total outage. Recall: excellent — fires on anything that even brushes the threshold.

The fatal flaw: terrible precision. A brief 0.1% error rate sustained for exactly 10 minutes triggers this alert while consuming only 0.02% of the entire monthly error budget. Taken to the extreme, a service could fire this alert up to 144 times a day, every single day, and still comfortably meet its SLO — the alert is reacting to noise, not genuine threat.
attempt 2

Widen the window

expr: job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate36h{job="myjob"} > 0.001 (alert only if 5% of the 30-day budget would be spent — a 36-hour window)

Detection time: still good — 2 minutes 10 seconds for a full outage. Precision: meaningfully better, since a sustained high rate is far more likely to represent a genuine threat.

The new flaw: terrible reset time. After a total outage that's already been fixed, the 36-hour rolling average stays elevated above threshold for the entire remaining 36-hour window — the alert keeps firing for a day and a half after the problem is already resolved, actively training on-call to distrust and eventually ignore it. Long windows are also more expensive to compute, given the sheer number of underlying data points involved.
attempt 3

Add a "for" duration instead

expr: job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1m{job="myjob"} > 0.001 for: 1h

The tempting shortcut: require the error rate to stay above threshold for a full hour before firing, using a cheap short-window rate rather than an expensive long-window average.

This is actively worse in a specific, dangerous way. Duration doesn't scale with severity — a total (100%) outage and a marginal 0.2% outage both take exactly one hour to alert, even though the 100% outage would burn 140% of the entire 30-day budget in that single hour alone. Worse: if the metric dips back under threshold even momentarily, the duration timer resets from zero — meaning an SLI that oscillates between passing and failing may never fire at all. The chapter's own striking example: 100% error spikes lasting exactly 5 minutes every 10 minutes, forever, never trip a 10-minute duration threshold — despite quietly consuming 35% of the entire monthly error budget. The chapter explicitly recommends against using duration clauses for SLO-based alerting, with only a narrow exception for filtering very short-lived noise.
attempt 4

Alert on burn rate

Burn rate is the key concept that finally breaks the tradeoff: how fast, relative to the SLO's own pace, the service is consuming its error budget. A burn rate of 1 means "consuming budget exactly fast enough to hit zero right at the end of the SLO window" — for a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, that's a constant 0.1% error rate.

Burn rate 1 → 0.1% error rate → exhausts budget in 30 days
Burn rate 2 → 0.2% error rate → exhausts budget in 15 days
Burn rate 10 → 1% error rate → exhausts budget in 3 days
Burn rate 1,000 → 100% error rate → exhausts budget in 43 minutes

The trick: instead of scaling the window to control precision, scale the threshold — using a short, cheap window, but requiring a proportionally higher error rate before it counts as significant. Deciding "a 5% budget spend over 1 hour deserves a page" works out to a burn rate of 36:

expr: job:slo_errors_per_request:ratio_rate1h{job="myjob"} > 36 * 0.001

Precision: good — you're now specifically targeting a meaningful chunk of budget. Detection time: good. Reset time: much better — 58 minutes, versus the previous approach's day-and-a-half.

The remaining flaw: poor recall. A sustained 35x burn rate — enough to exhaust the entire 30-day budget in just 20.5 hours — never actually crosses the 36x threshold this rule is watching for, so it never fires at all. And 58 minutes is still a genuinely long reset time for something already resolved.
attempt 5

Multiple burn rates, layered together

Rather than picking one single burn rate/window pair, layer several — each tuned to catch a different severity and urgency, with the fastest, most severe burns routed to a page and slower, more gradual burns routed to a ticket instead:

Budget consumedWindowBurn rateNotification
2%1 hour14.4Page
5%6 hours6Page
10%3 days1Ticket
expr: (rate1h > 14.4*0.001) or (rate6h > 6*0.001) severity: page expr: rate3d > 0.001 severity: ticket

The rationale for the ticket tier specifically: a slow-but-real burn (10% budget in 3 days) is genuinely significant, but the pace of consumption itself gives you comfortable time to address it during normal working hours — waking someone up at 3am for it would be a wasted interruption, not a necessary one.

Two real costs of this approach: reset time gets even longer thanks to the 3-day window, and — without care — multiple notifications can fire simultaneously for the same underlying event (10% of budget spent in 5 minutes technically also satisfies the 6-hour and 1-hour thresholds too), requiring genuine alert-suppression logic to avoid flooding on-call with three redundant pages for one real problem.
attempt 6 — the recommendation

Multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerts

The final refinement adds one more parameter: a second, much shorter window that must also currently be breaching the same threshold, alongside the long window, before the alert is allowed to fire. This is the piece that finally fixes reset time properly — once the short window drops back under threshold, the alert stops firing almost immediately, even while the long window is still catching up.

Rule of thumb for sizing the short window: roughly 1/12th the length of the long window. In the chapter's own worked example: after 15% errors for 10 minutes, the short-window average crosses the threshold almost immediately, and the long-window average crosses about 5 minutes later — at which point the alert actually starts firing. Once the errors stop, the short window drops back under threshold within about 5 minutes (reset time fixed!), even though the long window itself takes a full 60 minutes to fully recover — but because both conditions are now required together, the alert correctly stops as soon as the short window clears.
Page: (rate1h > 14.4*0.001 AND rate5m > 14.4*0.001) OR (rate6h > 6*0.001 AND rate30m > 6*0.001) Ticket: (rate24h > 3*0.001 AND rate2h > 3*0.001) OR (rate3d > 0.001 AND rate6h > 0.001)

Google's own recommended starting parameters for a 99.9% SLO:

SeverityLong windowShort windowBurn rateBudget consumed
Page1 hour5 minutes14.42%
Page6 hours30 minutes65%
Ticket3 days6 hours110%
The chapter's clear verdict: this approach achieves good precision, good recall (thanks to the 3-day tail), fast detection, and fast reset simultaneously — genuinely balancing all four dials at once, which none of the previous five attempts managed. The real cost is complexity: more parameters, window sizes, and thresholds to actually understand and maintain — which is exactly why the chapter later recommends bucketing services rather than hand-tuning every single one independently (see below).
low-traffic services

When a single request can blow your whole budget

The burn-rate math above assumes traffic volume is high enough that a single failed request is statistically negligible. That assumption breaks for genuinely low-traffic services — nights, weekends, or just inherently low-volume systems.

10 requests/hour, 1 fails → 10% hourly error rate
For a 99.9% SLO, that's a 1,000x burn rate → pages immediately
→ consumes 13.9% of the entire 30-day budget from ONE failed request
→ the whole month's budget only tolerates ~7 failed requests, total

Single requests routinely fail for ephemeral, uninteresting reasons that genuinely don't warrant the same emergency response as a systemic outage. Four concrete mitigations, matched to different situations:

  • Generate artificial traffic. Synthetic requests give the monitoring system a steady baseline signal to work with, reusing existing SLO logic and thresholds unchanged — but this only works well if you can synthesize a genuinely representative slice of real request types (harder for complex, stateful services), and it has a real blind spot: a real-user-affecting bug that the synthetic traffic happens not to trigger will hide behind the artificial traffic's own healthy-looking success rate.
  • Combine related low-traffic services into one monitored group. Works well when services genuinely share a failure domain (a common backend database, say) — pooling raises effective traffic volume, giving the same burn-rate math real statistical footing. The downside: a complete failure of one individual low-traffic service inside the group might not move the group's blended number enough to count as significant on its own.
  • Change the service or client to lower the real impact of a single failure. Client-side retries with exponential backoff and jitter, or fallback/store-and-forward paths that capture a failed request for later retry — both genuinely reduce how much a single ephemeral failure actually hurts the user, which is arguably the more fundamental fix regardless of traffic volume.
  • Just lower the SLO, or widen the alerting window. If a single failure's real user impact doesn't actually justify paging someone at 3am, a lower SLO reflects that honestly, and only pages for genuinely large, sustained outages. This is explicitly framed as a product decision, not a pure engineering one — lowering an SLO changes real expectations and shifts when the error budget policy actually kicks in, which needs the same stakeholder buy-in Chapter 2 describes for setting any SLO in the first place.
extreme targets

When the SLO itself is unusually loose or unusually tight

Very loose SLOs (e.g., 90% availability) can break the recommended default parameters in the opposite direction: a complete, total outage only consumes 1.4% of that budget in an hour — under the 2% page threshold from the standard table — meaning the "page" tier as written could literally never fire, regardless of how bad things get. These thresholds genuinely need re-tuning to the specific SLO in use, not applied as a universal constant.

Very tight SLOs create the opposite, more dangerous problem. A service targeting 99.999% monthly availability exhausts its entire budget from a total outage in just 26 seconds — faster than the collection interval of many monitoring systems, let alone the full round trip through alert evaluation, notification routing, and a human actually reading a page. Even an automated remediation system might not react in time to save any budget at all. The chapter's blunt conclusion: getting notified you have 26 seconds of budget left isn't a useful defense at this reliability tier — the only real defense is designing the system so a 100% outage is structurally, vanishingly unlikely to happen in the first place (staged/canary rollouts, covered in Chapter 16, are the concrete mechanism — rolling a change out to just 1% of users first buys roughly 43 minutes of reaction time at the same 1% burn rate, instead of 26 seconds at 100%).
scaling alerting

Don't hand-tune every service — bucket by request class instead

A service with 100 microservices (or, equivalently, one service with 100 distinct request types) accumulates real, unmanageable toil if every single one gets its own hand-picked window sizes and burn-rate thresholds. The chapter's clear recommendation: pick one set of alerting parameters and apply it uniformly — then, separately, group request types into a small number of availability tiers based on genuine similarity of importance, rather than custom-tuning each one individually.

ClassMeaningAvailabilityLatency (p90 / p99)
CRITICALMost important — e.g. user login99.99%100ms / 200ms
HIGH_FASTCore interactive functionality99.9%100ms / 200ms
HIGH_SLOWImportant, but not latency-sensitive (e.g. generating a report)99.9%1,000ms / 5,000ms
LOWNeeds *some* availability, but outages are mostly invisible (e.g. background notification polling)99%—
NO_SLONot user-visible at all — dark launches, alpha functionality——

This deliberately trades a small amount of fidelity (compared to a hypothetical, perfectly-tuned-per-endpoint system) for a dramatic reduction in real, ongoing management overhead — the chapter's judgment is that this handful of buckets still protects user happiness effectively, at a fraction of the cognitive cost of tuning parameters individually across dozens or hundreds of endpoints.
