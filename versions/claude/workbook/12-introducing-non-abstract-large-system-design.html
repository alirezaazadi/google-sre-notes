what NALSD is

Turning a whiteboard sketch into real numbers, at every step

Non-Abstract Large System Design is Google's name for the disciplined skill of assessing, designing, and evaluating large systems — combining capacity planning, component isolation, and graceful degradation into one repeatable process. The "non-abstract" part is the entire point: every system eventually runs on real machines, in real datacenters, over real networks — and skipping the exercise of turning a whiteboard design into concrete resource estimates makes it dangerously easy to design something that simply doesn't translate into reality. Doing this work up front, repeatedly, tends to produce far fewer painful last-minute design changes forced by some physical constraint nobody checked earlier.

The chapter's own important caveat: the specific numbers that come out of a NALSD exercise matter less than the soundness of the reasoning and assumptions that produced them. Early assumptions heavily shape the final numbers, and making *perfect* assumptions was never the goal — the real value comes from combining many individually imperfect-but-reasonable estimates into a genuinely better understanding of the design as a whole.

NALSD runs in two broad phases, each anchored by its own questions — asked roughly in order, though in practice engineers bounce between them constantly:

PhaseQuestions
Basic designIs it possible? (ignoring resource limits, what design would satisfy requirements?) Can we do better? (can it be meaningfully faster, smaller, more efficient — O(N) vs. O(ln N)?)
Scaling upIs it feasible? (does it fit real money/hardware constraints — does it need to go distributed?) Is it resilient? (does it fail gracefully — component failure, whole-datacenter failure?) Can we do better?
the problem

Designing a click-through-rate (CTR) dashboard for AdWords

The chapter's running example: build a system that accurately measures and reports CTR (clicks ÷ impressions) for every AdWords ad, sourced from two separate logs — a query log (every search, with the ad IDs shown) and a click log (every click, referencing the query it came from). Target SLOs: 99.9% of dashboard queries complete in under 1 second, and 99.9% of the time, displayed CTR data is under 5 minutes old. Assumed transaction rates: 500,000 search queries/sec and 10,000 ad clicks/sec — deliberately large numbers, chosen specifically to force genuinely distributed thinking rather than something a single beefy machine could plausibly handle.

iteration 1

One machine — cheap to reason about, and quickly disqualified

The simplest possible design: one machine, one SQL database, indexes on query_id and search_term, joining the two logs to compute CTR on demand.

Query log entry ≈ 2 KB (aggressively rounded up for safety)
(5×10⁵ queries/sec) × (8.64×10⁴ sec/day) × (2×10³ bytes) ≈ 86.4 TB/day
→ rounded to ~100 TB/day including index overhead

A 4TB HDD sustains ~200 IOPS
(5×10⁵ queries/sec) / (200 IOPS/disk) ≈ 2,500 disks just for query-log writes

Fitting 100 TB entirely in RAM (64GB/machine): 100TB / 64GB ≈ 1,563 machines
Two independent disqualifications, not just one. Even setting aside the sheer resource math, testing the design against "what happens when this component fails?" reveals a long list of single points of failure — CPU, disk, power, network, cooling — any one of which would blow the SLO the instant it happened. The math alone would have killed this design; the resilience question kills it even harder. This iteration wasn't wasted effort, though — it's what surfaced the real constraints (IOPS, RAM footprint) the next design needs to respect.
iteration 2

MapReduce — the obvious next step, disqualified by the SLO itself

A batch MapReduce job could periodically join accumulated query and click logs, producing per-ad_id CTR summaries. This scales horizontally without limit — more machines always let it finish, regardless of input size.

The batch model is fundamentally incompatible with the 5-minute freshness SLO. Running MapReduce in small enough batches to hit that window means arbitrary, non-overlapping batch boundaries — and if a query lands in batch 1 while its corresponding click lands in batch 2, the two never get joined at all. MapReduce handles genuinely self-contained batches well; this problem isn't shaped that way.
iteration 3

LogJoiner — an on-demand join, keyed the smart way

The key insight: clicks are far rarer than queries (2% of volume), so scale around the smaller stream instead of batching the larger one. Build a QueryStore holding the full query log, keyed by query_id, queryable on demand. A new component, LogJoiner, consumes the click-log stream continuously, looks up each click's corresponding query in QueryStore, and writes the joined result into a ClickMap (indexed by ad_id) — while a parallel QueryMap independently indexes all query impressions by ad_id, giving the dashboard both halves of the CTR ratio it needs. If a query can't be found for a click yet (log delivery lag), the click waits and retries up to a time limit, then is discarded.

LogJoiner network: (10⁴ clicks/sec) × (2×10³ bytes) = 20 MB/sec (~160 Mbps) QueryStore lookup: (10⁴ clicks/sec) × 8 bytes request + 2×10³ bytes response ≈ 160 Mbps LogJoiner → ClickMap: (10⁴ clicks/sec) × (10³ bytes) = 10 MB/sec (~80 Mbps) Total: ~400 Mbps — manageable on ordinary machines ClickMap: (10⁴ clicks/sec)×(8.64×10⁴ sec/day)×(16 bytes) ≈ 14 GB/day → round to 20 GB/day QueryMap: 3 ad slots × (5×10⁵ queries/sec)×(8.64×10⁴ sec/day)×(16 bytes) ≈ 2 TB/day
2 TB/day is small enough to store on one machine — but the IOPS problem from iteration 1 hasn't actually gone away. The write pattern is still too many small, frequent operations for a single disk to sustain. The fix isn't a faster disk — it's sharding the whole pipeline.
iteration 4

Sharded LogJoiner — parallelism, plus real fault tolerance

Run many LogJoiner instances in parallel, each handling one shard of the data. A log sharder routes each record deterministically: hash the query_id, take it modulo N (number of shards), and send the record to that shard. This means each LogJoiner sees only a consistent slice of traffic — and crucially, the same query and its corresponding click always land on the same shard, since both share the same query_id. QueryMap is sharded differently — by ad_id instead of query_id — since that's the key it's actually looked up by; ClickMap reuses the same sharding scheme as QueryMap, being the smaller of the two.

Reliability, addressed directly: what happens when a LogJoiner machine dies mid-join? The fix is deliberate, lightweight replication — the log sharder sends duplicate copies of each record to two separate shards, with those two replica shards explicitly placed on different machines. If one shard's machine fails, the other still has the joined data, and the system keeps serving at full accuracy. Losing both replicas simultaneously is possible but rare enough to be an acceptable risk absorbed by the SLO's own error budget — when it does happen, the logs can simply be reprocessed, and the dashboard shows slightly-older-than-5-minutes data for a brief window rather than failing outright.
One more single point of failure remains, and it's a big one. All of this still runs inside a single datacenter — if that datacenter itself goes offline (power, network, or worse), every ClickMap replica is gone simultaneously, and every user's dashboard goes dark at once. The sharding-and-replication work solved machine-level failure; it did nothing for datacenter-level failure.
iteration 5

Multidatacenter — consensus, and the real cost of surviving a datacenter loss

Surviving a full datacenter outage means ClickMap data has to be genuinely available in multiple, independent datacenters — without simply multiplying total compute cost by however many datacenters you deploy to. This is a direct instance of the distributed consensus problem covered in Chapter 23 of the original SRE book: replicate the service (3 or 5 copies) across datacenters, use a consensus algorithm like Paxos to keep them in reliable agreement even through a datacenter-sized failure, and accept that each write now costs at least one network round trip between participating nodes — a real, unavoidable throughput ceiling, though writes can still be parallelized across many independent shards to work around it.

Assumed Paxos round-trip latency across geographically separated datacenters: ~25ms → max ~40 sequential operations/sec per single Paxos-backed process Replication doubles effective traffic: 20,000 clicks/sec, 1,020,000 queries/sec Minimum tasks needed: (1.02×10⁶ queries/sec) / (40 ops/sec) ≈ 25,500 tasks Memory per task (2 copies of 2TB QueryMap ÷ tasks): 4×10¹² / 25,500 ≈ 157 MB/task Tasks per 64GB machine: 6.4×10¹⁰ / 1.57×10⁸ ≈ 408 tasks/machine Network: (1.02×10⁶ queries/sec) × (2×10³ bytes) ≈ 16 Gbps total Per task: 16Gbps / 25,500 tasks ≈ 640 Kbps/task Per machine: 408 tasks × 640 Kbps ≈ 256 Mbps (well within a 1Gbps NIC)
The payoff of all this arithmetic: roughly 64 machines per datacenter, at ~25% of each machine's network capacity. Sharded by ad_id across ~25,500 lightweight Paxos-backed tasks, each holding a genuinely small memory footprint, the multidatacenter design turns out to be entirely tractable — not the enormous, unaffordable multiplier a naive "just copy everything to every datacenter" instinct might have feared.

Checked against the original requirements: LogJoiner scales horizontally to absorb all click volume; QueryStore and QueryMap comfortably hold a full day's data at the assumed rate; the dashboard's actual read path (a lookup by ad_id against QueryMap and ClickMap) is fast and simple, satisfying the 1-second latency SLO; and because every component in the pipeline scales horizontally, the 5-minute freshness SLO can always be defended by simply adding more machines wherever the pipeline is currently the bottleneck.

the throughline

Iteration builds on iteration — nothing gets thrown away

The chapter's closing emphasis is genuinely important: each design iteration wasn't wasted work discarded once it failed — the one-machine iteration's IOPS and RAM math directly informed every later sharding decision; the MapReduce iteration's freshness failure directly motivated the on-demand LogJoiner design; the single-datacenter LogJoiner's resilience gap directly motivated the consensus-based multidatacenter design. NALSD lets a design evolve step by step, incorporating what each iteration reveals, rather than starting over from a blank whiteboard every time a flaw surfaces.

Splitting components by how each was actually expected to grow — LogJoiner by query_id, QueryMap and ClickMap by ad_id — is what let different parts of the system scale independently, and removed any single piece of hardware or software instance as a structural bottleneck. The chapter frames NALSD explicitly as a learned skill, not an innate one — it needs regular, deliberate practice to stay sharp, and the ability to move fluidly from an abstract requirement to a concrete, defensible resource estimate is treated as genuinely critical to building systems that stay healthy over the long run, not just at launch.
