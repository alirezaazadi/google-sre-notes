the premise

Change management as an explicit SRE responsibility

Ben Treynor Sloss, in the original SRE book's introduction, described SRE teams as characterized by "rapid innovation and a large acceptance of change," naming organizational change management as a core SRE responsibility. This chapter examines several established change-management theories through an SRE lens, then applies them retroactively to two real Google case studies.

Product teams exist to ship features fast, typically via "launch and iterate." SRE sits in the middle of that fast-moving landscape, balancing the real risk change introduces against product reliability — with the error budget (Chapter 2) as the primary mechanism making that balance concrete and negotiable rather than a constant tug-of-war.

the theories

Five frameworks, none universally applicable — but each genuinely useful somewhere

ModelCore ideaSRE fit
Lewin's Three-Stage ModelUnfreeze → change → freeze — persuade a group change is needed, execute it, then institutionalize the new patternMost useful for macro-level organizational planning
McKinsey 7-SStructure, strategy, systems, skills, style, staff, shared values — covers both business and people-management elements explicitlyUseful when shifting from traditional sysadmin to a holistic SRE approach
Kotter's Eight-Step ProcessNamed one of the most influential business books of all time — begins with establishing urgency, ends with institutionalizing the changeParticularly well-suited to SRE, since urgency is often already present by default — scaling and reliability crises rarely need to be manufactured
Prosci ADKARAwareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement — a people-centric framework balancing business and human sides of changeGenuinely useful for coordinating change across globally distributed teams, though operational time constraints make its iterative pacing hard to fully honor
Bridges Transition Model / Kübler-Ross Change CurveDescribe people's emotional reactions to change (the latter adapted from grief research)Not formal change-management frameworks themselves, but genuinely useful tools for any people manager — unhappy people are rarely productive
Deming Cycle (PDCA)Plan-Do-Check-Act — continuous, cyclical process improvement, common in DevOps (CI/CD adoption, e.g.)Not suited to genuine *organizational* change management — it ignores the human side entirely, and frequent org-chart churn actively saps confidence and culture
Google's own practical mapping: Kotter's process fits SRE teams that treat embracing change as a core responsibility; ADKAR is worth SRE management's attention specifically for coordinating change across distributed teams; and every individual SRE manager benefits from familiarity with the Bridges and Kübler-Ross models purely as people-support tools during turbulent periods, independent of which formal change model is actually driving the larger effort.
case study 1

Waze: from ad hoc grassroots response to Kotter's model, applied retroactively

Waze, a community-based navigation app Google acquired in 2013, kept operating with real startup-style autonomy post-acquisition even as active users, engineering headcount, and infrastructure grew rapidly. Waze's response to the resulting challenges was grassroots and technical — small engineer-led groups, not management-driven structured change — yet the sequence of what actually happened closely mirrors Kotter's eight steps, applied after the fact as a lens rather than a plan followed on purpose.

The message queue: replacing critical infrastructure without stopping the world

Every Waze component — real-time, geocoding, routing — communicated through a single shared message queue. As throughput grew, the system began failing under its own load, forcing SREs into shorter and shorter manual interventions. At its absolute worst, the entire Waze SRE team spent most of a two-week stretch firefighting around the clock, at one point restarting message-queue components hourly just to keep messages flowing for tens of millions of users. Because SRE also owned Waze's release pipeline, this firefighting directly throttled feature velocity — engineers drowning in operational fires simply had no bandwidth left to support new launches.

Kotter's urgency stage needed no manufacturing here — the crisis itself supplied it. Making the severity visible convinced Waze leadership to reprioritize and fund real reliability work. A small "guiding coalition" — two SREs plus a senior engineer — formed the strategic vision: a future where SRE toil was no longer needed just to keep messages flowing. They evaluated off-the-shelf message-queue products and concluded only a custom-built solution could meet Waze's genuine scale and reliability requirements.

Building a replacement in-house demanded near-term breathing room. The coalition removed that barrier by enlisting a volunteer army of developers from teams already using the old queue — each reviewed their own service's code to cut unnecessary message volume, rolled out a compression layer atop the existing system, and built a dedicated queue for one component alone responsible for over 30% of total traffic. These stopgaps bought roughly two months to build and test a working prototype of the new system.

The migration itself was handled by rebuilding client libraries so services could publish and receive on either or both systems simultaneously, switched centrally through a shared control surface — letting load shift gradually rather than all at once. The first real migration phase targeted low-traffic, high-importance flows where a messaging outage would be catastrophic — writing to both systems in parallel gave these flows a genuine backup path, and a couple of real near-misses (where that backup path kept core Waze services running while the old queue faltered) supplied concrete, visible wins justifying further investment. As SRE automated the migration process and the new system covered more use cases by default, migration velocity accelerated sharply — closing with Kotter's final step, institutionalizing the change: the old system was formally deprecated, stragglers migrated a few quarters later, and the replacement now handles over 1,000x the load of its predecessor with minimal ongoing manual intervention.

The next cycle: releases and deployment

Kotter's insight that change is genuinely cyclical, not a one-off event, played out directly: eliminating the message-queue bottleneck simply surfaced the next one. With firefighting behind them, Waze SRE found its sole ownership of the manual, infrequent, high-risk release process was now the binding constraint on development velocity — infrequent releases meant each one bundled a large delta of changes, which meant a much higher chance any single release needed a rollback.

Improvement happened incrementally, with no master plan from day one. A senior Waze developer built a standard "batteries included" microservices framework, and SRE partnered to bake in reliability-focused features (a shared control surface, automation-friendly default behaviors) — letting a quick, initially scrappy tool suite (built solo by one SRE over just a few days) manage what had previously been the most expensive parts of releasing. As more components split into microservices under this framework, the value of these tools became obvious org-wide, and SRE's per-release burden shrank correspondingly.

Even with the release process meaningfully improved, engineering leadership still wouldn't take on release ownership themselves — releases remained too burdensome. A small SRE/developer coalition sketched a further strategic vision: continuous deployment via Spinnaker, giving one-click builds and deployments across hundreds or thousands of microservices. Technically superior in every dimension — yet development teams still resisted adopting it, driven by two real factors: nobody wanted to be the one pushing their own releases to production, and general change aversion sharpened by poor visibility into what the new process was actually doing.

Waze SRE broke down that resistance by demonstrating concrete value, not by mandating adoption — a centralized dashboard tied release status directly to standard microservice metrics, giving development teams real confidence a deployment had actually succeeded. Working closely with a few volunteer, systems-savvy teams first produced visible wins proving the new system delivered genuinely more value than the old process, at which point engineering leadership finally set an org-wide goal: every team releases via Spinnaker. Org-wide training and dedicated consulting for complex cases supported the migration — and as early adopters found the new process faster and less painful than waiting on SRE to push releases for them, adoption accelerated on its own, with engineers now pressuring their own remaining unmigrated dependencies rather than blaming SRE for the delay. Today, over 95% of Waze's services deploy through Spinnaker with minimal human involvement — and because a new microservice built on the shared framework gets a trivially configured pipeline for free, new services have a strong built-in incentive to standardize on it from day one.

Lessons learned

Change management theory genuinely isn't wasted effort — viewing Waze's experience through Kotter's lens after the fact shows real applicability, and a more deliberate application of the model at the time could plausibly have streamlined the whole process further. Grassroots change like this needs close collaboration between SRE, developers, and real executive support — a small, focused, cross-functional coalition was the key ingredient at every stage, and over time these ad hoc groups can and should mature into formal, standing cooperation.

Incremental change is genuinely easier to manage than jumping straight to a "perfect" endpoint — which is usually infeasible anyway when the system is actively collapsing under you, and whose definition of "perfect" tends to shift as new information surfaces along the way. Iteration produces early, visible wins that build real organizational buy-in for continued investment — and if early iterations don't demonstrate value, you lose far less time and fewer resources abandoning the effort early rather than late. A broad, flexible master plan — goals stated at a high level, with room to adapt — is what actually makes incremental change navigable.

Sometimes existing infrastructure genuinely can't support your strategic vision, and building something new — expensive as that is — is worth it when it pushes you past a real local maximum rather than just patching symptoms. A useful forward-looking exercise: where will your current systems and tooling hit real bottlenecks as the business grows over the next few years? Anything that doesn't scale horizontally, or scales superlinearly against a core metric like daily active users, deserves serious redesign consideration well before it becomes a genuine crisis.

case study 2

Common tooling adoption: an organizational shift, viewed through ADKAR

Unlike Waze's crisis-driven story, this case study is a deliberate organizational evolution — no acute scaling emergency forced the issue — which makes ADKAR (balancing people and technical dimensions of change explicitly) a better analytical fit than Kotter's urgency-first model.

The problem: independently reinvented wheels, everywhere

Google SRE found itself running multiple, largely independent software solutions solving roughly the same problem across monitoring, releases/rollouts, incident response, capacity management, and more. This arose partly because tool builders were disconnected from real, current user needs in a production landscape that itself changes rapidly and constantly — and partly because consumer needs genuinely diverged ("this rollout must be fast, approximate is fine" versus "this rollout must be 100% correct, slow is fine"). Teams waiting for a hypothetical, fully-featured universal solution eventually gave up and built their own narrow tool instead; teams with genuinely specific needs resisted adopting a broader, less-tailored solution even when one existed. The long-term organizational benefit of consolidation was clear in the abstract, but no individual team was staffed or incentivized to simply wait for it.

Design: from a virtual, part-time team to a centralized, dedicated one

Framing this concretely: what if all Google SREs could use one common monitoring engine and shared dashboards, usable out of the box without heavy customization — and the same underlying logic applied to releases, incident response, capacity management, and beyond? From an initial pool of 65+ candidate projects, months of gathering real customer requirements, verifying roadmaps, and market analysis narrowed the scope to a handful of genuinely vetted priorities.

The first structural attempt — a virtual team of subject-matter experts spending 80% of their time on horizontal projects while staying on-call for their home teams — ran into real, specific friction. Coordinating a team fractured across regular on-call rotations and multiple time zones was genuinely hard, with a lot of mental state lost switching between running a service and building serious software. Consensus-building and code review both suffered from the lack of any shared location or common working hours. And headcount for horizontal work initially had to be pulled directly from existing teams' own capacity — creating real tension even within Google between maintaining today's systems and investing in tomorrow's infrastructure.

The redesigned approach dropped the 80/20 split entirely: most SRE software development now happens via small, physically centralized groups of senior, experienced-but-currently-heads-down engineers focused purely on building — with broader cross-org requirements still gathered via videoconference, email, and real travel. The team settled on a concrete adoption target: roughly 60% of Google engineers using SRE-built tooling, treated as a reasonable working definition of "almost everyone." In ADKAR terms, this redesign phase sits squarely between the knowledge and ability stages — the org had plenty of talented, knowledgeable engineers, but was effectively asking people used to thinking like SREs to instead think like product engineers focused on customer requirements, roadmaps, and delivery commitments, which took a genuine, deliberate structural change to actually enable.

Implementation: Viceroy and the "zero-config" layer

Viceroy (Chapter 31 of the original SRE book) began as a grassroots effort to build one unified monitoring dashboard solution, eventually growing into Google's de facto standard, at which point a dedicated centralized team assumed formal ownership. Even after Viceroy unified the underlying framework, real duplication remained — every team still had to decide what data to display and how to organize it, rebuilding largely similar custom dashboards independently. A second, parallel effort built an opinionated zero-config layer on top, assuming a service fit one of a handful of common organizational patterns and providing comprehensive standard dashboards automatically — with most services eventually migrating to these standard views, while genuinely unique or very large services retained the option to build fully custom ones.

Lessons learned

Migrating many small, interdependent systems into fewer, more general ones — without disrupting already-running services real customers depend on — turns out to be far harder engineering work than designing something equivalent from a genuinely blank sheet. Building genuinely horizontal, widely-adopted software demands deep, ongoing listening to prospective users — the actual work looks a lot more like product management than typical SRE work, and Google explicitly added product-management and user-experience talent specifically to fill that gap once it became clear.

The team's original assumption — "if you build something great, people will naturally flock to it" — turned out to be wrong. No matter how genuinely good a new tool was, the perceived cost of migrating away from something already familiar and working (however scarred by toil) was reliably experienced as very high. Individual engineers' honest internal reaction was often simply: "I'm not improving anything — I'm swapping one working piece for another." ADKAR names this precisely as the knowledge-to-ability gap: people genuinely need time, coaching, and hands-on training to move from understanding a change intellectually to actually being able to execute it.

The practical fix: migration cost needed to approach zero ("just recompile and you get the new thing automatically"), and the benefit needed to be immediately obvious and concrete ("now you're protected against vulnerability X") — to the team, to individuals, and to the company all at once. A second, equally important shift: SRE historically built internal tools on a "best-effort" basis, squeezed into whatever gaps existed between running production, capacity planning, and firefighting — meaning delivery timing was genuinely unpredictable, and consumers of these tools correspondingly trusted them less, sensing constant delay and a rotating cast of owners. Building common tooling for genuine broad adoption required treating adoption itself as the primary success metric, not just feature completeness — and required staffing these efforts like real, fully committed product teams, with predictable schedules and support comparable to any other engineering effort at Google, rather than perpetually deprioritized side projects.

How development itself was conducted shaped how it was perceived, more than the team initially expected. Projects gained real traction specifically once staffed by production-experienced engineers 100% dedicated to the effort, with transparent, predictable public communication ("we'll have X done by Y date") — people trusted the eventual product because they could observe, from early on, exactly how it was being built. Effective rollout required clear scope, advance visibility, evaluation against a genuinely wide range of real use cases (deliberately targeting the most skeptical potential adopters first, not the easiest ones), a decisive quality advantage over whatever it was replacing, and near-zero friction to actually adopt.

Adoption drives a genuine, compounding network effect: broader reach makes each incremental improvement more valuable, which justifies more development investment, some of which flows directly back into further lowering migration cost — reinforcing adoption further still, and eventually justifying long-term, dedicated team staffing rather than opportunistic side-project support. Useful ways to actually measure this kind of impact: how quickly can a new product engineer stand up a world-scale service using the shared tooling? How easily can an SRE move between domains, enabled by shared primitives? How many distinct services can be managed through the same common building blocks, as one coherent experience rather than many bespoke ones? All are genuinely valuable measures — but adoption itself has to be the first one you actually track.

the throughline

No single model fits every kind of SRE change — and that's fine

The Waze and common-tooling case studies tackle genuinely different problem shapes — an acute, crisis-driven technical scaling emergency versus a deliberate, longer-horizon organizational and cultural shift — which is exactly why no single formal change-management model cleanly covers the full spectrum any real organization will eventually face. Kotter's eight-step process and the Prosci ADKAR model both offer genuinely useful lenses, each suited to different circumstances rather than competing for the single "correct" answer. The one true constant across both stories: change in an environment as dynamic as SRE demands continuous reevaluation and iteration — many genuinely successful changes start as organic, grassroots efforts, but nearly all of them benefit from real structured coordination and planning as they mature past their earliest, most improvised stages.
