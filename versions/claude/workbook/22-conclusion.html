Conclusion: Onward…

predicting forward

"He who lives by the crystal ball soon learns to eat ground glass"

The Workbook's closing chapter opens with real, stated humility, quoting economist Edgar Fiedler on the danger of predicting the future — and then risks it anyway, offering a handful of genuine, hedged observations about where SRE seemed headed as of early 2018.

the pattern

The future belongs to the past — pent-up demand from everywhere

Since the first SRE book's publication, the authors observed a genuinely large, pent-up appetite for SRE practice from enterprises of every size. Large enterprises, contrary to the familiar stereotype that big organizations change slowly, showed a correspondingly large appetite to adopt SRE robustly — the chapter predicted real innovation from these firms in the following year. Smaller firms, meanwhile, found ways to adopt SRE practices without needing a full, globally-distributed dedicated team — confirming a long-held suspicion that the substance and sequence of SRE's practices matter, but nobody has to "eat the whole meal at once" to benefit meaningfully from parts of it.

The chapter also floats a genuine, direct invitation: if a reader saw a real market opportunity to build products or services helping companies adopt SRE, the authors explicitly encouraged pursuing it — even inviting readers to write in through O'Reilly and share their progress.

adjacent disciplines

SRE + <insert other discipline>

Many thoughtful readers suggested SRE principles should extend naturally into other disciplines — security chief among them. The chapter is candid that neither the original book nor this one covered SRE/security overlap in real depth, not out of oversight but because the authors genuinely weren't sure yet what they wanted to say about it. At the time of writing (early spring 2018), the term DevSecOps was just emerging in the marketplace — an explicit recognition that development, security, and operations are fundamentally interdependent — and the chapter flagged this directly as a genuinely promising, still-unexplored space for any SRE looking for new territory. More broadly, the chapter frames this as the start of a recurring pattern: repeatedly asking "how would SRE apply to X?" across an expanding range of fields, with real, sometimes genuine value likely waiting in several of them.

unexpected reach

Trickles, streams, and floods — including a team of lawyers

The volume of people wanting to contribute content to this second book meaningfully exceeded the first — expected, given how well-received the original was. What genuinely surprised the authors was the sheer variety of contributors who reached out. The chapter's own specific, memorable example: a team of lawyers approached the authors wanting to explore incorporating error budgets into nontechnical legal agreements — clearly out of scope for this particular book, but a real signal of interest from an entirely different professional population the authors hadn't anticipated at all.

Given that breadth, the authors were explicit that this volume was never meant to be simply an implementation companion to the first book — it was meant as a launching point for genuinely new streams of SRE-related content going forward: more books, articles, podcasts, videos, and formats not yet imagined. The Workbook was framed deliberately as a beginning, not an ending point.

correcting course

SRE belongs to all of us

The first SRE book's original motivation was straightforward: explain the specific practice that made Google work well for its users, and share that knowledge broadly. In doing so, though, the authors acknowledge they anchored heavily on their own direct experience — and in their enthusiasm to explain their own view of the world, they unintentionally alienated parts of the broader DevOps community, who felt their own contributions to the field had gone unacknowledged. This Workbook deliberately devotes real, substantial space (Chapter 1 especially) to directly addressing DevOps and SRE's actual relationship, specifically to correct that earlier omission.

By the time of this Workbook's writing, the authors expected the number of non-Google SREs to soon significantly outnumber Google's own. Whatever its specific institutional origin, SRE had become a genuine global community of practice, with Google simply one member among many — made visible through conferences like SREcon, meetups, and a growing body of outside publications, all part of an ongoing, mutual exchange of lessons the authors hoped this book would help extend further.
the closing note

A genuine, warm sign-off

The chapter closes on a note of real gratitude — describing the Workbook as a labor of love, and noting that the process of assembling it taught the authors themselves some genuinely surprising lessons about what SRE had become since the first book, leaving them excited rather than settled about what comes next. True to form for a group of SREs, they note they'll be running their own postmortem on the process of writing the book itself — with a wink that readers might eventually see it surface as a blog post somewhere down the line.

The book's final line, offered in its original form as the genuine closing sentiment of the entire two-volume project:

"May your queries flow and your pagers stay silent." — Dave, Betsy, Niall, Stephen, and Kent
this collection

What this closes out

This is the final file in the full SRE Books Explained collection — all 34 chapters of the original Site Reliability Engineering book (Parts I–V) and all 21 chapters plus this conclusion from The Site Reliability Workbook. Together they trace the full arc: SRE's founding philosophy and organizational principles, the practices that keep production healthy day to day, and the processes that let an SRE organization itself grow, mature, and adapt over time — from a single engineer setting an SLO with no dedicated SRE staff at all, up through globally distributed teams spanning continents and disciplines well beyond Google's own walls.
