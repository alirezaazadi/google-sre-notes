Managing Incidents

Incident management makes coordination an explicit system. The chapter contrasts a technically focused but unmanaged outage with a structured response using Incident Command, Operations, Communication, Planning, a recognized command post, a living incident document, and explicit handoffs.

01 — unmanaged

Why good engineers can collectively make an outage worse

The fictional service begins losing datacenters: one, then two, then three of five. Remaining sites overload. Mary, the on-caller, becomes absorbed in logs and rollback. Other engineers independently “just look,” leadership asks repeatedly for ETAs, and another expert deploys an uncoordinated CPU-affinity change that restarts servers and worsens the failure.

Nobody is malicious or incompetent. The failure comes from sharp focus on the technical problem, poor communication, and freelancing. The incident exceeds the cognitive capacity of one technical responder, but the organization has not changed modes.

02 — roles

Separate responsibilities recursively

Google's approach is based on Incident Command System principles. The Incident Commander holds the high-level state, priorities, and team structure. They implicitly own any role not yet delegated.

The Operations lead coordinates technical changes; importantly, Ops should be the group actually modifying the system. The Communications lead provides periodic internal/external updates and may maintain status artifacts. Planning handles longer-horizon work: bugs, handoffs, logistics, and tracking how production diverged from normal so it can be restored later.

If one role overloads, it can delegate subproblems. Structure creates autonomy because each person knows what they own and does not have to second-guess every other responder.

03 — command post

Create a place where the incident exists

Responders and stakeholders need a recognized interaction point—a physical war room, chat/IRC channel, or equivalent. The communication stream becomes an operational log and reduces side-channel changes.

A live incident state document keeps the most important information at the top: current impact, roles, known facts, active hypotheses/actions, timeline, and next priorities. It survives person changes and later becomes evidence for the postmortem.

04 — handoff

Leadership transfer must be explicit

Long incidents cross shifts and time zones. The incoming commander should receive a direct state transfer, acknowledge that they now own command, and the change should be announced to the rest of the response.

An ambiguous handoff can create two commanders—or none. Reliability practices treat ownership transitions as a state change requiring confirmation.

05 — managed version

The same outage with structure

In the managed version, Mary recognizes the incident is expanding and asks Sabrina to take command while Mary remains focused on operations. Sabrina records state and communicates; additional people are recruited into defined workstreams rather than independently editing production.

The organization can now pursue diagnosis and mitigation in parallel while shielding technical responders from repeated status requests. Incident management does not make engineers smarter; it makes their collective capacity usable.

06 — when

Declare before chaos is obvious

Formal structure has some overhead, so not every minor page needs a full command system. But rapidly expanding scope, unclear duration, multiple teams, significant user/business impact, or several parallel workstreams are strong signals to declare.

Starting slightly early is cheaper than trying to impose structure after communication and ownership have already fragmented.

07 — incident lifecycle

Management begins before the outage becomes obviously huge

The chapter’s operational lesson is to establish an incident-management structure while the event is still manageable. Waiting until dozens of engineers have joined a chat means the team has already incurred coordination debt.

Declare the incident, name the person coordinating it, separate operational work from communication, and maintain one authoritative incident state. From then on, every new responder can be placed into an explicit work stream instead of independently reconstructing the entire event.

When leadership changes, handoff must be explicit: current impact, active hypotheses, actions in flight, risky changes, and next communication deadline all need to transfer. Otherwise the new leader appears to have authority but lacks the incident’s state.
