Google Site Reliability Engineering Book · Chapter 1

Introduction

The opening chapter explains why Google created Site Reliability Engineering: traditional operations tends to scale by adding people as operational work grows, while SRE deliberately applies software engineering to production so service growth does not require proportional growth in manual operations.

01 — sysadmin model

Why the traditional development/operations split becomes expensive

The chapter starts from the ordinary sysadmin model: assemble existing components, deploy them, keep them running, and respond to changes and failures. This model is easy to understand and has a large existing tool and talent ecosystem. Its weakness appears as the service grows: if handling changes and incidents remains manual, the operations team must grow roughly with the operational workload.

The indirect cost can be larger. Development and operations are separated by skills, vocabulary, incentives, and risk assumptions. Developers are rewarded for launching features; operators holding the pager are rewarded for stability. Because change causes many outages, release discussions can become a trench war of gates and workarounds rather than a shared engineering problem.

02 — definition

What Google means by SRE

Google’s alternative is to hire engineers to run production who can also write software that replaces manual operations. Ben Treynor Sloss summarizes the idea as the result of asking a software engineer to design an operations function.

The historical staffing model described in the chapter is intentionally mixed: roughly 50–60% software engineers and 40–50% engineers with strong systems expertise who also have enough programming ability to automate. That combination matters because SRE must understand both the code and the operating environment.

Core distinction: SRE is not “operations plus some scripts.” The organization reserves real engineering capacity to remove recurring operational work and improve the production system.
03 — 50%

The 50% rule keeps SRE from degenerating into pure operations

Google places an upper bound of about 50% of SRE time on operational work. At least half the time should go to engineering that reduces future toil, improves reliability/performance/utilization, or adds capabilities needed to run the service.

This is both an organizational control and an employment promise. Toil expands naturally: pages, tickets, deployments, quota changes, and manual fixes all have immediate urgency. Without a protected engineering share, they can consume 100% of the team's capacity and remove the very work needed to make the system scale.

Simple staffing thought experiment:
8 SREs × 40h/week = 320h/week.
If operations consumes 200h, operational share = 200 / 320 = 62.5%.
That is not merely a busy week if persistent—it means the SRE model is losing the engineering capacity that should reduce future work.
04 — tenets

The recurring responsibilities of SRE

The chapter introduces the themes developed by the rest of the book: availability, latency, performance, efficiency, change management, monitoring, emergency response, and capacity planning. These are connected rather than separate specialties.

Monitoring should produce three broad outputs: a page when a human must act immediately, a ticket when action is needed but can wait, and logging when information is useful for later diagnosis but does not itself demand action.

Emergency response focuses strongly on mean time to repair. The chapter notes that a responder following a prepared playbook can have roughly one-third the MTTR of a responder improvising without one. Change management matters because changes are a dominant outage trigger; the book cites a rough figure of about 70% of outages being caused by some kind of change.

05 — error budgets

Resolve the stability-versus-velocity argument with data

SRE rejects “maximum possible reliability” as the universal objective. Product owners and SRE define an appropriate service target; the difference between that target and perfection is an error budget that can be spent on launches, experiments, and change.

When reliability is comfortably inside the objective, the organization can move faster. When the budget is exhausted, continued risky change becomes hard to justify and engineering attention shifts toward reliability. This aligns product and operations around a shared number instead of incompatible instincts.

Product chooses reliability target
Measured service performance
Compute remaining error budget
Budget healthy → accept change risk
Budget exhausted → reliability first
06 — scale

What 'site reliability' is ultimately optimizing

SRE wants the operational cost of running a service to grow more slowly than the service itself. Automation, self-healing, reliable release processes, good monitoring, capacity planning, and postmortem-driven engineering are all mechanisms toward that goal.

A service that requires a human to manually intervene for each unit of growth has not achieved that scaling property, even if today's uptime looks excellent.
