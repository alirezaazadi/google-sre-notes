Google Site Reliability Engineering Book · Chapter 11

Being On-Call

On-call is a production interface between humans and the service. Google manages it as an engineering system: response times must match SLOs, rotations must leave enough engineering time, incident frequency must be low enough for proper follow-up, and responders need psychological safety, escalation paths, and enough production exposure to stay competent.

01 — duty

What an on-caller actually does

The on-call engineer manages urgent outages and may also vet/perform production changes. Response expectations depend on the service: the chapter gives typical values of about 5 minutes for highly time-critical/user-facing systems and 30 minutes for less time-sensitive services.

Primary and secondary rotations are common. One team may use secondary only as a fallback for missed pages; another may split responsibilities so primary handles pages and secondary handles nonurgent production activity.

02 — response time

Availability math constrains human response

A 99.99% (“four nines”) quarterly availability target allows only around 13 minutes of downtime in a quarter. That makes a half-hour human response incompatible with the objective unless the system automatically masks most failures before a human is required.

The more stringent the objective, the more response must shift toward automatic detection, failover, mitigation, and very fast escalation. Humans cannot compensate for an architecture whose failure budget is smaller than the time needed to notice and understand the failure.

Four-nines intuition:
quarter ≈ 90 days ≈ 129,600 minutes
0.01% downtime ≈ 12.96 minutes.
So a service relying entirely on a human who might respond in 30 minutes cannot realistically achieve that target.
03 — quantity

Keep on-call from consuming the engineering role

SRE aims for at least 50% engineering time. Of the remaining half, the chapter uses a guideline of no more than 25% on-call, leaving up to another 25% for other nonproject operational work.

If two people must always be on-call (primary + secondary), that 25% rule implies a minimum of roughly eight engineers for a single-site week-long rotation. For dual-site teams, about six engineers per site is a reasonable lower bound in the chapter's model.

Follow-the-sun reduces unhealthy night work, but multisite operation adds communication and coordination cost. Staffing is therefore a reliability parameter, not merely a management preference.

04 — quality

Count incidents, not only hours

The chapter estimates that one on-call incident—mitigation, root-cause work, remediation, postmortem, and bug fixes—takes about six hours on average. That leads to a target ceiling of roughly two incidents per 12-hour shift. The median should often be zero.

If one component pages daily, the service already has no room for the unrelated incident that will eventually occur. Persistent paging above the target requires engineering work, reduced scope, or other corrective measures.

05 — safe thinking

Reduce stress so responders can think deliberately

High-stakes systems can push engineers toward fast intuitive reactions. Complex outages often need slower deliberate reasoning. Clear procedures, well-tested tools, blameless culture, and safe escalation reduce the stress that causes reckless production changes.

Escalation is a skill, not a failure. A responder should pull in expertise when the incident exceeds their system knowledge, risk authority, or available attention.

06 — underload

Too few incidents can also weaken reliability

A rotation that almost never handles production can become rusty: engineers forget commands, architecture, escalation paths, and practical failure behavior. That is operational underload.

Exercises such as disaster tests and scenario drills maintain readiness. The ideal on-call load is neither constant firefighting nor total disconnection from production.
