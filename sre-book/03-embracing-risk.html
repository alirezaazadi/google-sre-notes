Google Site Reliability Engineering Book · Chapter 3

Embracing Risk

SRE does not seek the maximum possible reliability. It chooses a reliability level that matches user and business needs, quantifies the resulting tolerance for failure, and deliberately trades some risk for development speed, cost efficiency, and product value.

01 — not 100%

Extreme reliability can make the product worse

The chapter opens with a counterintuitive claim: beyond a point, more reliability is worse for users because the engineering cost comes out of features, performance work, technical-debt reduction, or lower prices. Users also experience less reliable components such as their devices and networks, so they may not perceive a difference between extremely high service levels.

SRE therefore aims for a service that is reliable enough, but no more reliable than needed. The availability target behaves conceptually like both a minimum and a ceiling: consistently exceeding it by a huge margin may mean the organization is spending opportunity cost users do not value.

02 — risk cost

Reliability has direct and opportunity cost

Direct cost includes redundant compute, extra replicas, storage parity, spare capacity, geographically separate sites, and other infrastructure needed to survive failures.

Opportunity cost is engineering time spent eliminating tiny residual risks instead of delivering user-facing value or removing more important technical debt. The reliability curve is nonlinear: each additional increment near perfection can cost dramatically more than the previous one.

03 — measure

Make risk comparable using availability

Service risk includes trust, revenue, brand, and harm, but those are difficult to combine into one operational metric. Google commonly uses unplanned downtime / availability as a tractable proxy. For globally distributed serving systems, request-based availability can be more meaningful than declaring the whole service simply up or down.

Request-based availability is typically successful well-formed requests divided by total well-formed requests. This naturally represents partial failure: losing 1% of traffic for an hour is not the same event as losing every request for that hour, even if both involve a component outage.

Example request availability:
successful requests = 9,995,000
valid requests = 10,000,000
availability = 9,995,000 / 10,000,000 = 99.95%.
Unavailability (risk consumed) = 0.05%.
04 — choose target

Business context decides the acceptable risk

Factors include how essential the product is, whether users have alternatives, what failure costs the business, expected user behavior, service maturity, and dependency needs. A batch report can tolerate a very different outage pattern from an authentication system.

Not all errors have equal consequence. A missing optional image and a privacy violation cannot sensibly be treated as identical merely because both are “failed requests.” Some failure modes justify intentionally shutting down service rather than risk incorrect or unsafe behavior.

05 — error budget

Risk tolerance becomes permission to change

Once a service has an objective, the allowed gap from perfection becomes an error budget. That budget creates explicit risk-taking: releases and experiments can consume some reliability without immediately triggering a stability freeze.

If budget is depleted, reliability work becomes a rational business response because the organization has exceeded the level of user risk it agreed to accept. This is the mechanism that turns abstract risk philosophy into release policy.

100% perfection is costly
Choose user/business target
Target leaves error budget
Spend budget on useful change
Budget exhausted → reliability work
06 — important nuance

Optimize user happiness, not the uptime number

The ultimate objective is not a particular number of nines. It is user value across reliability, features, latency, price, and trust. The nines are a tool for making one dimension of that trade-off explicit.

This framing also disciplines infrastructure teams: if clients genuinely need a stronger reliability tier, expose that requirement and price/engineer it deliberately rather than making every dependency silently assume infinite availability.

06 — expected loss

Risk becomes useful when it can be compared

The chapter frames risk as something to reason about quantitatively rather than as a binary 'safe/unsafe' label. A low-probability failure with catastrophic consequences can justify more engineering than a frequent low-impact error.

A rough expected-loss model can be written as probability of the event × impact if it happens. The exact units might be failed requests, revenue, user minutes, or another business-specific measure. The point is not false precision; it is to make competing reliability investments comparable.

Error budgets perform a similar normalization for routine reliability: they translate an abstract target into a concrete amount of failure the service can absorb during an interval.

Simple risk lens: expected impact ≈ probability of failure × cost when it occurs. Use this to compare mitigations, not to pretend uncertain inputs are exact.
07 — risk classes

Availability is only one dimension of risk

The chapter also reminds readers that service risk is not captured by one uptime percentage. Serving private data publicly, corrupting durable state, returning a wrong result, and briefly refusing a request can have radically different consequences.

This is why reliability targets should be tied to real service semantics. In a safety-sensitive situation, intentionally refusing service may be the correct response because availability is less important than preserving confidentiality or integrity.

SRE therefore asks not merely 'how many failures?' but 'what kind of failure is this, who experiences it, and what is the acceptable trade-off?'
