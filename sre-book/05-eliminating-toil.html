Eliminating Toil

The book gives “toil” a precise SRE meaning: production work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, has little enduring value, and grows with service scale. Keeping toil bounded is what allows SRE staffing to scale sublinearly and remain an engineering function.

01 — definition

Toil is not simply unpleasant work

Administrative overhead, difficult cleanup, or a one-time manual investigation is not automatically toil. The chapter names a cluster of properties: manual, repetitive, automatable, tactical, no enduring value, and O(n) with service growth.

Manually running a script still counts as toil for the human hands-on time. A first-time novel task usually is not toil. If human judgment is the essential part, the work may be operational but not automatable toil.

02 — enduring value

Ask what is different after the work

A useful test is whether the service is permanently better when the task finishes. Cleaning up a broken alerting configuration may be tedious, but if it removes future noise it has enduring engineering value. Repeating the same capacity change by hand every week leaves the service in essentially the same operational state and is much closer to toil.

Toil that grows linearly with users or traffic is especially dangerous. An ideally designed service should absorb large growth—often an order of magnitude—without needing proportionally more recurring human steps.

03 — 50%

Why Google enforces a toil ceiling

Google advertises the goal that operational work remain below roughly 50% of SRE time. The other half is for engineering projects that reduce future toil or improve reliability, performance, utilization, and service capability.

The chapter shows how on-call creates a floor: in a six-person rotation where each engineer spends one week primary and one week secondary per six-week cycle, 2/6 ≈ 33% of time is already potentially committed to interrupt/on-call work before additional tickets or manual procedures are counted.

On-call floor example from the chapter:
6-person rotation; 1 primary week + 1 secondary week per 6 weeks
potential operational share = 2 / 6 = 33%.
That leaves limited room before crossing the 50% total toil target.
04 — consequences

What excessive toil does to the organization

Toil consumes the engineering time needed to remove toil, producing a feedback loop. It also causes career stagnation, burnout, morale problems, slower development, and increased human-error risk.

A pure operations queue may still keep the current service alive, but it cannot achieve SRE's main scaling advantage: making operational demand grow more slowly than the service.

05 — reduction

Automate or design the work away

Measure recurring work first, then prioritize high-frequency/high-cost/high-risk targets. Before automating the exact human procedure, ask whether the procedure itself can be simplified or eliminated.

A self-service product interface, automatic control loop, better default, or architectural change can be better than a script. Automation has maintenance cost and failure modes of its own, so the goal is lasting operational leverage rather than maximum code generation.

06 — quantify it

Calculate the toil floor instead of arguing from feelings

The chapter makes the 50% engineering target concrete by looking at an ordinary on-call rotation. If an SRE spends one week as primary and one week as secondary in every rotation cycle, some operational work is unavoidable before tickets, releases, and other interrupts are counted.

In a six-person rotation, those two on-call weeks create a lower bound of 2/6, or about 33%, of the engineer’s time potentially exposed to toil. In an eight-person rotation, the corresponding floor is 2/8, or 25%. This is why rotation size is not merely a staffing comfort issue—it directly affects the amount of time left for engineering.

Google’s quarterly SRE surveys cited in the chapter reported average toil around 33%, while individual values varied widely. The point is not to optimize everyone to an identical percentage, because toil is spiky. The point is to detect teams or individuals that remain far above the engineering/toil balance over a long period.

6-person rotation: 2 on-call weeks ÷ 6-week cycle = 33% potential floor. 8-person rotation: 2 ÷ 8 = 25%.
Important distinction: software engineering, systems engineering, toil, and administrative overhead are separate categories. A grungy one-time cleanup that permanently improves the service can still be engineering; a clever script you manually run every day can still be toil.
07 — toxicity

Why too much toil is an organizational reliability problem

Small amounts of repetitive work are not automatically harmful. They can provide quick wins and production familiarity. The chapter’s concern is what happens when toil becomes the dominant mode of work.

At the individual level, excessive toil causes career stagnation, boredom, low morale, and burnout. At the team level, it slows feature and reliability progress, makes SRE’s role ambiguous, creates a precedent for other teams to offload more manual work, and drives attrition. It also breaks the promise made to engineers that SRE is an engineering discipline rather than a traditional operations queue.

The practical implication is that a persistent toil problem is not solved by praising people for working harder. It should trigger engineering work, workload redistribution, simplification, or a change in service ownership.
