Google Site Reliability Engineering Book · Chapter 26

Data Integrity: What You Read Is What You Wrote

Data integrity is stricter than ordinary uptime: users need their data to remain correct, accessible, and recoverable even when software bugs, operators, hardware, or entire sites fail. The chapter emphasizes restore-oriented SLOs, defense in depth, independent backups, fast recovery, early corruption detection, and the rule: trust storage systems, but verify.

01 — user view

Data loss, corruption, and prolonged unavailability can look identical

Users judge integrity from whether their data appears accessible and correct. A UI bug that shows an empty mailbox can destroy confidence even if the underlying bytes still exist.

After a 2011 Gmail recovery incident, the chapter suggests 24 hours as a useful starting threshold for 'too long' data unavailability in Google Apps-like products.

Integrity therefore spans more than durable disks: it includes accessibility, correctness, recovery time, metadata and every layer needed to present the user's data.

02 — stricter than uptime

Why 'four nines of good bytes' is catastrophic

Availability and integrity percentages do not have the same practical meaning.

The chapter contrasts 99.99% uptime—which leaves roughly an hour of downtime over a year—with 99.99% good bytes in a 2 GB artifact. The latter permits about 200 KB of corruption, enough to make a database, executable or document unusable.

Data integrity often therefore requires something effectively much closer to 100% correctness while allowing a bounded period of unavailability for recovery.

Book example: 0.01% corruption × 2 GB ≈ 0.0001 × 2,000 MB ≈ 0.2 MB ≈ 200 KB. '99.99% correct bytes' sounds excellent as a percentage but can represent catastrophic corruption.
03 — availability trade

Detect quickly, remove bad data, repair, and restore

If an artifact is corrupted once per year but the corruption is detected immediately, the artifact can be taken offline, repaired, and returned within about 30 minutes. Its yearly availability remains about four nines while its accessible data can remain essentially correct.

This motivates the chapter's central strategy: proactive detection plus rapid repair can trade a small amount of availability for much stronger integrity.

Do not continue serving known-corrupt data merely to preserve an uptime graph.

04 — backups

Practice restores, not just backup creation

Backup creation is easy to neglect because it consumes resources without visible daily benefit. The chapter reframes the problem around the visible capability the backup buys: restoration.

Teams should define data-availability SLOs for relevant failure scenarios and periodically demonstrate that they can actually meet those objectives by restoring.

A backup that is never restored is only evidence that a backup job ran, not that recovery works.

Operational rule: treat the restore path as a production feature. Measure it, exercise it, and keep it compatible with current code/schema.
05 — failure space

Root cause × scope × rate

The chapter classifies data-loss scenarios along three dimensions. Root cause includes user action, operator error, application bugs, infrastructure defects, hardware, and site catastrophe. Scope ranges from one user/entity to widespread loss. Rate ranges from sudden 'big bang' corruption to slow creeping loss.

Different combinations require different restoration strategies. A site fire and a bug deleting ten rows per minute for weeks cannot be recovered with the same mechanism.

A study of 19 Google recovery efforts found common user-visible losses from software bugs causing deletion or broken referential integrity, with especially difficult incidents involving low-grade corruption discovered weeks or months after introduction.

06 — defense in depth

Independent layers reduce correlated failure

Replication protects against individual hardware loss, but software can faithfully replicate a bad delete or corrupt mutation to every replica.

Backups therefore need enough independence—from live serving state, administrative paths, and sometimes physical failure domains—that the same event does not destroy both primary and recovery data.

Checksums and validation detect silent corruption, while restricted destructive operations, delayed deletion, audit trails and point-in-time history improve recoverability.

07 — restore speed

A technically complete backup can still be operationally useless

Recovery time depends on dataset size, restore bandwidth, indexing/reconstruction work and validation. The chapter describes strategies that parallelize restores horizontally and restrict them to relevant time/data slices to reduce otherwise enormous wall-clock time.

A restoration plan must be designed for the SLO, not merely for eventual completeness.

Older backups can also become harder to use as schemas and code change. Recovery testing catches these compatibility failures before a real incident.

08 — early detection

Corruption propagates, so time-to-detect changes recoverability

Bad data is copied, referenced and transformed. The longer corruption exists, the more downstream state depends on it and the harder it is to choose a clean restore point.

High-velocity systems complicate integrity with schema migrations, evolving service integrations, aging code and data split across stores.

Strongly consistent storage does not remove the application's responsibility to validate its own invariants. The chapter's memorable rule is: trust storage systems, but verify.

Application-level integrity checks, reconciliation jobs and anomaly detection are essential because even theoretically sound distributed algorithms are implemented by real software with bugs and operational edge cases.
