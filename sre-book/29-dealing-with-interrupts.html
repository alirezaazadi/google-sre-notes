Google Site Reliability Engineering Book · Chapter 29

Dealing with Interrupts

Pages, tickets, and ongoing operational responsibilities are necessary work, but distributing them randomly across engineers destroys cognitive flow. The chapter recommends polarizing time: designate people for interrupt work, let others remain genuinely noninterruptible, hand over responsibilities cleanly, and continuously reduce the root causes of interrupts.

01 — categories

Three kinds of operational load

The chapter groups operational load into pages, tickets, and ongoing operational responsibilities.

Pages have urgent response expectations measured in minutes. Tickets require human action but usually have response windows in hours, days or weeks. Ongoing work includes pushes, flag rollouts and ad hoc customer requests that may not have formal SLOs but still interrupt focus.

Different teams assign these categories to primary on-call, secondary on-call, a dedicated ticket role, or ad hoc engineers. The chapter argues that the human productivity cost should be a first-class factor in that design.

02 — flow

Context switching costs much more than the interruption duration

Focused engineering benefits from cognitive flow: clear goals, sustained concentration, immediate feedback and a sense of control.

An engineer attempting project work while also being continuously interruptible often gets neither the satisfaction of focused operations nor the productivity of focused engineering.

The chapter explicitly assigns substantial cost to context switches: a 20-minute interruption can realistically destroy a couple hours of productive project time because the engineer must leave and later reconstruct complex mental state.

Context-switch illustration from the chapter's reasoning: one 20-minute interruption includes two context switches. If returning to deep context consumes substantial recovery time, the practical loss can be measured in hours rather than 20 minutes. Therefore schedule structure matters more than simply counting ticket minutes.
03 — polarize

Do one mode of work at a time

The core recommendation is to polarize time: make a person clearly responsible for interrupt work for a long block—ideally a week, but a day or half-day can help—while other engineers are protected for project work.

When primary on-call is assigned, write that period off for deep project progress. If a project cannot tolerate the engineer disappearing for the on-call week, someone else should cover the shift.

A quiet pager can be paired with tickets or cleanup work that is easy to abandon. A busy primary should not also be expected to deliver project milestones.

04 — ticket role

Do not randomly spray tickets across the team

Random ticket assignment makes every engineer potentially interruptible at every moment, defeating the purpose of supposedly protected project time.

If ticket volume needs one person, make tickets a full-time role for that period. If it needs two, staff two—not the entire team a little bit.

Centralized ticket responsibility also reveals the real queue size. If unofficial helpers quietly pick up tickets, management may incorrectly conclude that one-person staffing is sufficient.

05 — handoffs

Ongoing responsibilities should belong to roles, not individuals forever

Multiweek rollouts or push responsibilities often follow the original engineer even after their interrupt shift ends.

Instead, define roles such as push manager and formalize handoff so the next interrupt owner can continue the operation.

This turns hidden personal ownership into explicit team-owned state and protects the former on-caller's make time.

06 — reduce

Analyze the interrupt stream itself

Teams often survive each on-call/ticket rotation like a gauntlet and then forget it until their next turn. That pattern preserves the same recurring work forever.

Review pages and tickets as classes. Find repeat generators, fix the underlying issue, or deliberately silence the alert while the planned fix is underway when that is safe.

Policy can reduce toil too. Ask requesters to provide required diagnostics or perform nonprivileged preparation steps themselves. A service that cannot justify enough developer attention to fix chronic operational pain may need to be deprecated, replaced, or have its pager returned.

The objective is a respectful contract: customers do reasonable legwork and provide complete information; SRE provides a useful and timely response.
