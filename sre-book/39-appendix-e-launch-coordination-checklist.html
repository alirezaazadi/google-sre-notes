Appendix E — Launch Coordination Checklist

The original launch checklist is a compact map of production risk. Its value is not checking boxes mechanically; it forces a launch team to reason about architecture, failure domains, capacity, failover, monitoring, security, change mechanisms, growth, dependencies, and rollout before real users exercise the system.

01 — architecture

Understand the serving shape first

Document the architecture, server types, client/request types, and programmatic clients.

Identify machine/datacenter layout, bandwidth, redundancy, network QoS, domain/DNS requirements and how users are routed.

A launch reviewer should be able to trace the important request paths and name the critical dependencies.

02 — capacity

Estimate launch spike and future growth

Estimate HTTP traffic, bandwidth, traffic mix and launch spike, not just steady-state average.

Load-test end to end and calculate per-datacenter capacity at the maximum acceptable latency.

Consider storage growth and the launch's impact on shared services.

The checklist also asks about spare capacity, 10× growth, bottlenecks, linear-scaling assumptions, caching and sharding/resharding.

03 — failure

Walk through failure domains explicitly

Ask what happens when a machine dies, a rack fails, a cluster disappears, or the network between datacenters fails.

For every service-to-service dependency, define detection of backend death, restart/termination behavior, load balancing, rate limiting, timeouts, retries and errors.

Backup/restore and disaster recovery are launch-readiness concerns, not post-launch extras.

04 — observability

Monitor the service and the monitoring

Cover internal state and end-to-end behavior, alert management, financially important logs/signals, and the health of monitoring itself.

The memorable line 'monitor the monitoring' captures a real dependency: an outage detector that silently stops collecting data can create false confidence.

05 — change

Canaries and repeatable release are part of readiness

Define controlled ways to update binaries, data and configuration.

Use repeatable builds, live-traffic canaries, staged rollout and change controls.

Manual tasks need explicit procedures because launch-day improvisation creates a human single point of failure.

06 — dependencies + schedule

Plan for things you do not control

External services may see launch spikes too. Define monitoring, capacity expectations, graceful degradation, and controls that prevent your launch from overrunning a partner.

Hard deadlines and public events require a rollout schedule with enough room for observation and rollback. The checklist even prompts teams to think carefully about risky timing such as Mondays/Fridays and to document standard operating procedures.
