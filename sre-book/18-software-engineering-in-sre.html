Software Engineering in SRE

SRE engineering projects are real products for internal users. They arise from firsthand production pain and aim to create reusable leverage—safer rollout, monitoring, capacity planning, automation—rather than one-off scripts. The Auxon capacity-planning case illustrates why production expertise can become software infrastructure.

01 — why SRE writes software

Production expertise creates a unique product perspective

SREs operate some of the most complex parts of Google production and therefore understand requirements that may be invisible to a general application developer: fleet scale, graceful degradation, quota/capacity behavior, cross-infrastructure integration, and how tools are used during incidents.

The direct users of SRE-developed tools are often other SREs, producing high-signal feedback and allowing early versions to focus on functionality even if the user interface is still minimal.

02 — project quality

Not a home-directory script

The chapter distinguishes full software-engineering projects from quick hacks. An SRE tool should have a roadmap, ownership, testing, release discipline, observability, and consideration of its users. If it controls production, its failure modes may be as important as the service it manages.

This is essential to the SRE growth model: services supported by SRE are intended to grow faster than SRE headcount. Persistent automation and tooling are how a roughly linear-size organization can manage superlinear service growth.

03 — organizational value

Engineering projects help both production and SRE careers

Long-term projects balance interrupt/on-call work and keep software-development skills active. They also attract engineers with different backgrounds and reduce organizational blind spots.

The mixed software/systems population is valuable because software projects need deep awareness of operational reality while production response benefits from software-engineering approaches.

04 — Auxon

Capacity planning as a software problem

The Auxon case study begins from a recurring difficulty: capacity planning across many services is too complex and too important to perform by repeatedly hand-editing spreadsheets and assumptions. Demand forecasts, resource usage, service topology, and provisioning decisions must be kept consistent.

Automating that workflow creates not just labor savings but a common model. The tool can validate inputs, expose gaps, make plans reproducible, and help service owners reason about future growth with production data.

This is representative of high-leverage SRE software: turn repeated operational judgment into a system that retains the best parts of that judgment while making it scalable.

05 — design

Internal automation still needs product engineering

  • Identify the actual user and workflow before choosing an implementation.
  • Prefer APIs/data models that can support multiple services rather than hard-coded local assumptions.
  • Model partial failure and retries because production control tools rarely operate in an atomic world.
  • Make dangerous actions staged, reviewable, observable, and reversible.
  • Measure whether the tool actually reduces toil, pages, capacity misses, or change risk.
06 — leverage

The right metric is future operations removed

Example leverage estimate:
capacity plan takes 4 engineer-hours/service/month × 60 services = 240h/month.
A common tool cuts hands-on time to 45 min/service = 45h/month.
Gross recurring saving ≈ 195 engineer-hours/month, before counting fewer planning errors.
SRE software mindset: build the thing that makes a class of future operations cheaper, safer, and more consistent.
07 — product mindset

Internal reliability software needs users, interfaces, and maintenance

SRE engineering projects often start from a production pain point, but a durable solution should be treated like a software product rather than an elaborate personal script. Identify who will use it, which operations it makes safer, how failures are surfaced, how it is deployed, and who owns it after the original project ends.

The chapter’s examples illustrate why this matters at Google scale: capacity planning, configuration and fleet-management tooling must work across many services and teams. A generalized system can create far more leverage than repeatedly solving the same operational problem locally.

The success metric should therefore include future operations removed: hours of toil eliminated, pages prevented, safer changes enabled, or additional service scale handled without proportional staffing.
