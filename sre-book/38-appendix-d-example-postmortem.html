Appendix D — Example Postmortem

The Shakespeare Sonnet++ postmortem demonstrates how incident evidence becomes long-term reliability work. It quantifies impact, separates trigger from root/contributing causes, explains detection and resolution, records what went well/wrong/lucky, and creates prevention, mitigation, process, and policy action items with owners.

01 — event

What actually happened

The example service was down for 66 minutes during an extreme surge in interest after discovery of a new Shakespeare sonnet. The postmortem estimates 1.21 billion lost queries.

Traffic increased about 88×. Users searched for a term absent from the corpus, activating a latent resource leak. The combination of unprecedented load and the bug produced cascading failure across search backends.

The trigger was sudden traffic; the root cause was not 'too much traffic' alone. It was the interaction between exceptional traffic, an absent search term, the file-descriptor leak, and insufficient overload protection.

02 — resolution

Mitigation and permanent fixes are distinct

Responders directed traffic to a sacrificial cluster, increased capacity dramatically, updated the index so the pathological query no longer hit the leak path, and then fixed the file descriptor bug.

The postmortem says capacity was increased by roughly 10× as part of mitigation while public interest remained high.

Temporary mitigation restored service; source-code and overload-control changes reduce recurrence.

03 — action items

A good postmortem changes several layers

Actions include updating the cascading-failure playbook, improving cross-cluster load balancing, scheduling a cascading-failure test during DiRT, investigating continuous indexing, fixing the resource leak, adding load shedding, creating regression tests for 'queries of death', deploying the fixed subsystem, and freezing normal production changes because the error budget was exhausted.

This mix is important: not every action is 'fix the bug.' There are prevention, mitigation, testing/process, operational-readiness and policy responses.

04 — learning

What went well, what went wrong, where luck helped

Monitoring rapidly detected HTTP 500s and the team could distribute an updated corpus quickly.

The team was out of practice responding to cascading failure and blew the availability error budget by orders of magnitude.

They were lucky that an expert mailing list had the new sonnet text, logs contained useful stack traces pointing at file-descriptor exhaustion, and updating the index removed the query pattern that triggered the bug.

Writing down luck exposes protections the system currently lacks. A future incident should not need the same lucky conditions.

05 — timeline

Why a detailed timeline matters

The timeline shows the causal and response sequence: traffic 88× at 14:53, outage at 14:54, pager storm at 14:55, incident declaration at 15:01, leak diagnosis around 15:18, reindex completion at 15:20, sacrificial-cluster/capacity changes after 15:21, and continued testing/recovery.

These timestamps let reviewers evaluate detection latency, declaration timing, investigation paths and mitigation effectiveness without relying on retrospective impressions.
