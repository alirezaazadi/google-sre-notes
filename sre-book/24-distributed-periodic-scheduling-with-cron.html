Distributed Periodic Scheduling with Cron

Cron looks trivial on one machine but becomes a distributed-systems problem when schedules must survive machine failure and launching a logical job requires multiple remote operations. The chapter focuses on durable schedule state, leader election, partial launches, idempotency, ownership, and the semantics of duplicate versus missed executions.

01 — single host

Why ordinary cron is deceptively simple

Traditional crond runs on one machine, reads a crontab, and launches commands at specified times or intervals.

On a single machine, its state and failure semantics are relatively easy to understand: if the host is down, jobs may be delayed or missed; if the daemon restarts, it can reload the schedule.

At datacenter scale, the scheduler must remain available when individual machines fail and must launch jobs through another distributed system such as Borg.

02 — semantics

Missing a run and running twice are different kinds of failure

Many cron jobs have side effects. Sending a newsletter, performing payroll, deleting expired data, or moving money is not automatically safe to repeat.

After a crash, a distributed scheduler may know that it started a launch but not whether all external RPCs completed. Retrying can duplicate work; not retrying can miss work.

Therefore the design must make explicit whether a job is idempotent, whether duplicate execution is acceptable, and whether the external scheduler exposes enough state to determine what actually happened.

03 — dependencies

Keep a foundational scheduler's dependency set small

The Google cron service stores only a small amount of schedule/launch state. The chapter argues that putting this state in a large general distributed filesystem can add high-latency small writes and creates a dependency on infrastructure whose failure may already be affecting the datacenter.

Base infrastructure such as cron should have few critical dependencies so it remains useful during partial datacenter failure.

Replicas should also be placed in diverse physical locations so a single power distribution failure cannot remove the whole cron service.

04 — leader

One leader decides launches; followers synchronously learn the decision

A leader replica initiates due launches. Before or during the launch, followers synchronously update the next scheduled launch time and track the launch as open.

If the leader disappears, a follower is elected. The chapter notes that election should converge faster than about one minute so scheduled work is not missed or unreasonably delayed.

The new leader then inspects all open launches and resolves any that were left halfway through.

Important: leader election alone is not enough. The difficult state is the set of operations the old leader may have partially performed before it died.
05 — partial failure

Two synchronization points delimit a logical launch

Google's design records when a launch is about to begin and when it has finished. If the leader dies between those points, the new leader knows that the launch is ambiguous and must reconcile it.

To recover correctly, at least one property is needed: external operations are idempotent and can safely be repeated, or their state can be queried unambiguously.

The chapter describes precomputing stable names for datacenter jobs and distributing those names to all cron replicas. After leader failure, the new leader can look up each expected job name and launch only the missing ones.

06 — practical load

Scheduling itself can create synchronized demand

Cron naturally aligns jobs on human-friendly boundaries such as the top of the hour. At scale, thousands of jobs starting together can create a thundering herd against schedulers, storage and downstream services.

Distributed scheduling therefore needs concurrency controls, distribution/jitter of work where semantics allow it, and monitoring of job lateness and launch success.

The deeper lesson is that time-based coordination is a source of correlated load.

Scheduling intuition: 12,000 jobs all starting at 00:00 create 12,000 near-simultaneous launch operations. Spreading the same jobs uniformly across a 10-minute window averages only 20 launches/s. This is an illustrative smoothing calculation; whether you may jitter a job depends on its scheduling contract.
